1.2 What are graphics hardware and output devices?

Graphics hardware means the physical computer components used to create, process, store, input and display graphical information. Examples include a graphics processor, memory, monitor, printer, plotter, mouse, scanner and digitizer.

An output device receives processed information from a computer and presents it in a form that people can understand. In computer graphics, the output is commonly presented in two forms: hardcopy and softcopy.

Figure 1: Two broad forms of graphical output.

1.3 Hardcopy devices

A hardcopy device produces a physical and usually permanent copy of computer output. The result can be held, stored, shared or viewed without needing a computer screen. Examples include printed documents, engineering drawings, maps, charts and photographs.

The two important hardcopy output devices in computer graphics are printers and plotters.

A. Printers

A printer is an output device that transfers text, images or graphics from a computer onto paper or another print medium. Printers are commonly classified according to whether the print head physically strikes the paper.

1. Impact printers

An impact printer produces output by physically striking an inked ribbon against paper. The impact transfers ink onto the paper. Because parts physically hit the paper, these printers are generally noisy.

  • Dot-matrix printer: A print head contains a set of small pins. Selected pins strike an ink ribbon and form characters or images as a pattern of dots. It can print multipart forms but usually produces lower-quality graphics than modern non-impact printers.
  • Daisy-wheel printer: Characters are formed on the spokes of a wheel. The wheel rotates to position a character, and a hammer strikes it against the ribbon and paper. It produces clear text but is slow and unsuitable for complex graphics.
  • Line printer: Prints a complete line at a time, often using a chain or drum mechanism. It is designed for high-volume text output rather than detailed graphical images.
Remember: Impact means physical contact or striking. Dot-matrix, daisy-wheel and traditional line printers are common examples.

2. Non-impact printers

A non-impact printer creates output without the print mechanism striking an inked ribbon against the paper. These printers are generally quieter and can produce high-quality text and images.

  • Inkjet printer: Sprays very small droplets of liquid ink through tiny nozzles onto paper. It is commonly used for colour documents and photographs. Print quality depends on ink, paper and printer resolution.
  • Laser printer: Uses a laser or equivalent light source to form an electrostatic image on a photosensitive drum. Toner powder adheres to the charged image and is transferred to paper, where heat and pressure fuse it. It is fast and suitable for sharp text and high-volume printing.
  • Thermal printer: Uses heat to create an image on heat-sensitive paper or to transfer ink from a ribbon. It is common in receipts, labels and point-of-sale systems.
  • Electrostatic printer: Uses electrical charges to attract toner to selected areas of a printing surface. It is used in specialised high-speed printing systems.

B. Plotters

A plotter is a hardcopy output device designed to produce large, precise drawings such as maps, engineering plans, architectural layouts and technical diagrams. Traditional plotters draw lines by moving a pen or drawing mechanism relative to the paper. Modern large-format devices may use inkjet technology but are still commonly called plotters in practical use.

Types of traditional plotters

  • Flatbed plotter: The paper is held flat on a drawing surface. The pen mechanism moves in the horizontal and vertical directions to draw the required image. It is useful when accurate drawings are needed on a fixed sheet.
  • Drum plotter: Paper is wrapped around a rotating drum. The drum moves the paper in one direction while the pen moves across it in another direction. This allows long drawings to be produced.

1.4 Softcopy display devices

A softcopy device presents output electronically, usually on a screen. The displayed information is temporary: it can change or disappear when the screen is updated or the device is switched off. Common examples are monitors and projectors.

A display device is an output device that presents text, images, video and graphical information visually. In computer graphics, a display is important because it allows the user to inspect and interact with generated images.

1.5 Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) display

A Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) is a display technology that uses a beam of electrons to illuminate a phosphor-coated screen. CRT monitors were widely used in older computer systems and televisions.

Main parts of a CRT

  • Electron gun: Produces a beam of electrons.
  • Control grid: Controls the intensity of the electron beam and therefore the brightness.
  • Focusing system: Focuses the electron beam into a narrow spot.
  • Deflection system: Changes the direction of the beam so it reaches the required position on the screen.
  • Phosphor-coated screen: Emits light when struck by the electron beam.

Working principle

  1. The electron gun generates electrons.
  2. The focusing system forms them into a narrow beam.
  3. The control grid regulates beam intensity.
  4. The deflection system directs the beam to the required screen position.
  5. When the beam strikes the phosphor coating, the phosphor emits visible light.
  6. The beam scans the screen according to the display system being used. The image is maintained by refreshing the screen or by the storage property of a specialised display tube.

Figure 5: Components of a cathode ray tube. Source: TutorialsPoint — Cathode Ray Tube.

Advantages of CRT

  • Good motion handling and fast response.
  • Can provide good colour reproduction and viewing angles.
  • Historically available in a wide range of resolutions.

Limitations of CRT

  • Bulky and heavy because of the tube.
  • Consumes more power than many modern flat-panel displays.
  • Produces more heat and occupies more desk space.
  • Can suffer from geometric distortion and screen flicker if poorly adjusted.

1.6 Liquid Crystal Display (LCD)

A Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) uses liquid-crystal material to control how much light passes through each pixel. The liquid crystals do not normally produce their own light. An LCD therefore needs a light source behind or around the panel, or it may use reflected ambient light in some designs.

Basic working principle

  1. A light source provides illumination (in a typical monitor, this is a backlight).
  2. Polarising filters control the direction of light entering and leaving the panel.
  3. Liquid-crystal molecules change their orientation when an electric field is applied.
  4. This changes how much light passes through the pixel's optical layers.
  5. Colour filters combine red, green and blue subpixels to produce different colours.

Figure 6: LCD screen technology. Source: Linsn LED.

Advantages

  • Thin and lightweight compared with CRT.
  • Generally consumes less power than a comparable CRT.
  • Produces sharp images at its native resolution.
  • Does not use a large electron-beam tube.

Limitations

  • Image quality can depend on viewing angle and panel type.
  • Native resolution is important; scaling to other resolutions can reduce sharpness.
  • Some LCD panels can show motion blur or slower pixel response.
  • Backlight leakage or uneven illumination may occur.

1.7 Light Emitting Diode (LED) displays

In everyday monitor terminology, an LED monitor usually means an LCD panel that uses LEDs as its backlight instead of older fluorescent lamps. The image-forming layer is still liquid crystal. This is why many products described as LED monitors are technically LED-backlit LCDs.

There are also direct-view LED displays in which LEDs themselves emit light to form the image. Large outdoor video walls commonly use this arrangement. Do not confuse these direct-view LED screens with the usual LED-backlit LCD monitor.

Figure 7: LED display diagram (image found via Google Images).

Advantages of LED-backlit LCDs

  • Can be thinner and more energy-efficient than older CCFL-backlit LCDs.
  • Can provide high brightness.
  • LED backlighting can support local dimming in suitable designs.
  • Long service life in normal use.

Limitations

  • Quality varies with backlight design and panel technology.
  • Some edge-lit models can have uneven brightness.
  • Local dimming and contrast performance depend on the number and control of backlight zones.

1.8 Plasma display

A plasma display panel is a flat-panel technology in which tiny cells contain an ionised gas. Applying an electrical voltage causes the gas to produce ultraviolet energy, which excites phosphors in the cell. The phosphors then emit visible red, green or blue light to form the image.

Working principle

  1. The screen contains many small cells arranged as pixels.
  2. Each cell contains a gas mixture between glass panels.
  3. An electrical discharge ionises the gas and produces ultraviolet radiation.
  4. The ultraviolet radiation excites the phosphor coating.
  5. The phosphor emits visible light, and red, green and blue subpixels combine to create colour.

Figure 8: Plasma display panel. Source: GeeksforGeeks — Plasma Display Panel.

Advantages

  • Good contrast and deep black levels in many designs.
  • Wide viewing angles.
  • Good motion reproduction.

Limitations

  • Higher power consumption than many LCD displays.
  • Can generate more heat.
  • Risk of image retention or burn-in when static images remain for long periods.
  • Heavy panels and limited current availability; plasma is now a legacy display technology.

1.10 Important display terms

Pixel

A pixel (picture element) is the smallest addressable picture element in a raster image or display. A screen image is made from a grid of pixels. Each pixel stores or receives information that determines its displayed colour and brightness.

Resolution

Resolution describes the number of pixels used to represent an image or screen, commonly written as width × height. For example, 1920 × 1080 means 1920 pixels horizontally and 1080 vertically.

Total pixels = Horizontal pixels × Vertical pixels

Example: A 1920 × 1080 display contains 2,073,600 pixels (about 2.07 million pixels).

Refresh rate

Refresh rate is the number of times a display updates its image in one second. It is measured in hertz (Hz). A 60 Hz display updates 60 times per second. Refresh rate is not the same as resolution: resolution counts pixels, while refresh rate describes how often the image is updated.

Persistence

Persistence is the time for which a phosphor continues to emit light after the electron beam has moved away. If persistence is too low, flicker may become noticeable unless the screen is refreshed sufficiently often. If persistence is too high, moving images may leave trails.

Aspect ratio

Aspect ratio is the ratio of display width to display height. For example, 16:9 is a common widescreen ratio.

Aspect ratio = Display width : Display height

1.13 Exam-ready answers

2-mark answer — Define hardcopy and softcopy.

Hardcopy is the physical and permanent form of computer output, such as a printed document produced by a printer or a drawing produced by a plotter. Softcopy is the electronic form of output displayed temporarily on a monitor or projector.

5-mark answer — Differentiate between impact and non-impact printers.

Impact printers print by physically striking an inked ribbon against paper. They are generally noisy. Examples include dot-matrix, daisy-wheel and line printers. Non-impact printers produce output without striking the paper with a print head. They are generally quieter and can provide better print quality. Examples include inkjet, laser and thermal printers. Impact printers are useful for multipart forms, whereas non-impact printers are widely used for documents, images and photographs.

5-mark answer — Write a short note on plotters.

A plotter is a hardcopy output device used to produce large and accurate drawings such as maps, engineering plans and architectural designs. Traditional plotters draw lines using a pen mechanism. A flatbed plotter keeps the paper stationary while the pen moves in two directions. A drum plotter wraps paper around a rotating drum; the drum and pen move in different directions to create the drawing. Plotters are useful in CAD and other applications requiring precise large-format graphics.

5-mark answer — Explain the working of a CRT.

A Cathode Ray Tube is a display device that uses an electron beam to produce images on a phosphor-coated screen. The electron gun generates electrons. The control grid regulates beam intensity, the focusing system narrows the beam, and the deflection system directs it to the required screen position. When the beam strikes the phosphor coating, light is emitted. By controlling the beam position and intensity, text and graphics are formed on the screen.

15-mark answer — Explain different display technologies.

Introduction: Display devices are softcopy output devices used to present text, images and graphics generated by a computer. Important display technologies include CRT, LCD, LED and Plasma.

1. CRT: Uses an electron gun to produce a beam that strikes a phosphor-coated screen. Its main parts are the electron gun, control grid, focusing system, deflection system and phosphor screen. It offers good motion handling but is bulky, heavy and power-hungry.

2. LCD: Uses liquid-crystal material to control the passage of light through pixels. A backlight, polarising filters, liquid-crystal layer and colour filters work together to form the image. LCDs are thin and lightweight, but viewing angle and response depend on panel technology.

3. LED: A typical LED monitor is an LCD panel that uses LEDs as its backlight. It can be thin, bright and energy-efficient. Direct-view LED displays are different because the LEDs themselves produce the image.

4. Plasma: Uses tiny gas-filled cells. Electrical discharge produces ultraviolet energy that excites phosphors to emit visible light. Plasma displays offer good contrast and viewing angles, but consume more power and can suffer from image retention.

Conclusion: CRT and plasma are largely legacy technologies, while LCD and LED-backlit LCD displays are widely used. Each technology differs in its image-forming principle, power consumption, size, viewing angle and image quality.

1.15 Quick revision

  • Hardcopy = physical output; softcopy = electronic display output.
  • Printers and plotters are hardcopy devices.
  • Impact printers strike an inked ribbon; non-impact printers do not.
  • Dot-matrix is an impact printer; inkjet and laser are non-impact printers.
  • Traditional plotters are used for accurate large drawings; flatbed and drum are two types.
  • CRT uses an electron beam and a phosphor-coated screen.
  • LCD controls light using liquid crystals and normally needs a backlight.
  • A typical LED monitor is an LCD with LED backlighting.
  • Plasma uses gas discharge and phosphors to produce light.
  • Resolution counts pixels; refresh rate counts updates per second.

1.16 Common mistakes to avoid

  • Do not write that a typical LED monitor is completely unrelated to LCD. Most consumer LED monitors are LED-backlit LCDs.
  • Do not classify an inkjet printer as an impact printer. It sprays ink without striking the paper.
  • Do not say that a plotter is only used for text. Its key role is precise graphics and technical drawings.
  • Do not confuse resolution with refresh rate. One describes pixel count; the other describes update frequency.
  • Do not describe plasma as an LCD. Plasma uses gas discharge and phosphors; LCD uses liquid crystals to control light.
  • Do not claim all LED displays use an LCD panel. Direct-view LED displays exist too.

2.1 Learning objectives

After studying this topic, you should be able to define raster scan display, explain line-by-line scanning, understand pixels and scan lines, describe horizontal and vertical retrace, explain refresh and refresh rate, define the frame buffer, explain raster display architecture, and distinguish progressive from interlaced scanning.

2.2 What is a Raster Scan Display?

A Raster Scan Display represents an image as a rectangular grid of small picture elements called pixels. The display scans the screen one horizontal row at a time, beginning at the top and moving towards the bottom. It scans across the first row, returns to the beginning of the next row, and repeats until the complete screen has been covered.

As the display scans each row, it uses the stored pixel values to determine the colour and brightness of each pixel. In a traditional raster CRT, the electron beam is switched on where light is required and blanked during retrace. Modern LCD/LED panels do not use an electron beam, but they still use raster-based pixel addressing and refresh.

Figure 1: Raster scan display. Source: GeeksforGeeks — Raster-Scan Displays.

Example: A photograph shown on a monitor is represented by many pixels. The display updates those pixels in an organised raster pattern to show the complete picture.

2.3 Important Terms

A. Pixel (Picture Element)

A pixel is the smallest addressable element of a raster image or display. Every pixel has a position in the grid and stores or receives information that determines its brightness and/or colour. A pixel can be identified by its row number and column number.

Figure 2: A raster image is stored as a two-dimensional grid of pixels.

B. Scan Line

A scan line is one horizontal row of pixels on the display. The system scans the first row, then the second, and continues until the last row.

C. Frame

A frame is one complete image covering the entire display area. It contains all scan lines needed to represent that image.

D. Frame Buffer / Refresh Buffer

The frame buffer, also called the refresh buffer, is a dedicated memory area that stores the pixel information for the image. It stores intensity or colour values for all pixels. The display system reads these values in order and updates the screen.

2.4 Working of Raster Scan Display — Step by Step

  1. Image information is prepared: The computer or graphics system creates the picture information.
  2. Pixel values are stored: The colour or intensity value for every pixel is stored in the frame buffer.
  3. Scanning starts at the top-left: The display begins with the first pixel of the first scan line.
  4. Horizontal scanning: Pixels are read and displayed from left to right across the first row.
  5. Horizontal retrace: At the end of a row, the beam in a traditional CRT is blanked and returns to the left side to begin the next row.
  6. Next scan line: The process repeats for each row, moving from top to bottom.
  7. Vertical retrace: After the last scan line, the beam returns to the top-left corner to begin the next frame.
  8. Refreshing: The same frame is refreshed repeatedly so that the image appears stable.

Figure 3: Horizontal and vertical retrace. Source: GeeksforGeeks — Raster-Scan Displays.

2.5 Horizontal Scan and Vertical Scan

A. Horizontal Scan

Horizontal scanning is the movement across one scan line, from the left side of the display to the right side. In a traditional CRT raster display, the beam is active during the visible part and its intensity changes according to the pixel values.

B. Horizontal Retrace

Horizontal retrace is the return movement of the electron beam from the right end of a scan line back to the left side, where the next scan line begins. The beam is blanked during this movement so that an unwanted visible line is not drawn.

C. Vertical Retrace

Vertical retrace is the return movement after the last scan line has been completed. In a traditional CRT, the beam is blanked and returns from the bottom of the screen to the top-left position to begin the next frame.

Horizontal retrace = Right end of current row → Left start of next row
Vertical retrace = End of last row → Beginning of first row
Remember: Horizontal retrace happens after every scan line. Vertical retrace happens after the complete frame.

2.6 Refreshing and Refresh Rate

The light produced by a CRT phosphor gradually fades after the electron beam moves away. Therefore, the same image must be drawn again and again. This repeated updating of the complete screen is called refreshing.

The refresh rate is the number of complete frames displayed or refreshed in one second. Its unit is Hertz (Hz).

Refresh rate (Hz) = Number of complete frames refreshed per second

Example: If a display refreshes 60 complete frames in one second, its refresh rate is 60 Hz. If it refreshes 120 frames in one second, its refresh rate is 120 Hz. A low refresh rate can cause visible flicker, particularly on older CRT displays. The actual refresh rate supported depends on the display and its settings.

2.7 Frame Buffer

Frame buffer (refresh buffer) is a memory area that stores the pixel information of the image to be displayed on the screen.

2.8 Architecture of a Raster Scan Display System

A raster display system contains several connected components. The CPU or host computer issues graphics commands. The graphics/display processor may convert geometric descriptions into pixel values through scan conversion. The resulting pixel data is stored in the frame buffer. The display or video controller reads the buffer in the required order and sends signals to the display device.

Figure 4: Architecture of a raster system with a fixed portion of system memory reserved for the frame buffer. Source: SlideServe — Raster-Scan System. The source diagram is reproduced from the linked educational slide; see the source page for the complete context.

2.9 Advantages of Raster Scan Display

  • Realistic images: Can display photographs, filled regions, shading and colour gradients.
  • Colour support: Supports a large range of colours when sufficient bits are stored per pixel.
  • Complex scenes: Can represent detailed images using a pixel grid.
  • Common technology: Raster representation is used in televisions, monitors, phones and digital image systems.
  • Pixel-level representation: Every pixel has a definite row and column position.

2.10 Disadvantages of Raster Scan Display

  • Memory requirement: The frame buffer must store information for all pixels, so high resolution and colour depth require more memory.
  • Pixelation: Enlarging a raster image can reveal individual pixels and make edges look jagged.
  • Resolution dependence: Sharpness depends on the number of available pixels and the display's native resolution.
  • Refresh workload: The display repeatedly updates the frame, even when much of the image is unchanged.

2.11 Applications of Raster Scan Display

  • Desktop and laptop monitors.
  • Television screens.
  • Smartphones and tablets.
  • Digital photographs and image editing.
  • Video playback, games and multimedia systems.
  • Graphical user interfaces and modern computer applications.

2.13 Exam-ready Answers

2-mark answer — Define Raster Scan Display.

A raster scan display is a display system in which an image is represented as a grid of pixels. The screen is scanned horizontally, one row at a time, from top to bottom. Pixel intensity or colour values are stored in a frame buffer and used to produce the displayed image.

5-mark answer — Explain the Working of Raster Scan Display.

In a raster scan display, the image is represented by a rectangular grid of pixels. The pixel values are stored in a frame buffer. The display begins at the top-left corner and scans the first row from left to right. After completing a row, it performs horizontal retrace to return to the left side for the next row. This process continues from top to bottom. After the last row, vertical retrace returns the beam to the top-left corner. The complete image is repeatedly refreshed so that it appears stable.

5-mark answer — Explain Frame Buffer.

A frame buffer, also called a refresh buffer, is a memory area that stores the pixel information of the image to be displayed on the screen.

15-mark answer — Explain Raster Scan Display System with its Architecture.

Introduction: A raster scan display represents an image as a grid of pixels and scans the screen one horizontal row at a time from top to bottom.

Working: Pixel values are stored in a frame buffer. The display system reads these values and scans each row from left to right. After each row, horizontal retrace returns the beam to the left. After the last row, vertical retrace returns it to the top. The frame is repeatedly refreshed to maintain a stable image.

Architecture: The host CPU issues graphics commands. A graphics processor performs graphics operations and scan conversion. Pixel values are stored in the frame buffer. The video controller reads the buffer in the required order and generates display signals. The monitor presents the final image.

Refresh: Refreshing is the repeated updating of the complete screen. Refresh rate is measured in hertz and indicates the number of frames refreshed per second.

Advantages: Raster scan can display realistic images, colour, shading and complex scenes. It is widely used in modern display systems.

Disadvantages: It requires memory for all pixels, can show pixelation when enlarged, and image sharpness depends on resolution.

Conclusion: Raster scan is widely used for displaying pixel-based images and is fundamental to modern computer graphics.

2.15 Quick Revision

  • Raster scan represents an image as a grid of pixels.
  • The display is scanned one horizontal row at a time, from top to bottom.
  • A pixel is the smallest addressable element of a raster image.
  • A scan line is one horizontal row of pixels.
  • Frame buffer / refresh buffer stores pixel intensity or colour values.
  • Horizontal retrace returns the beam from right to left after each row.
  • Vertical retrace returns the beam from bottom to top after a complete frame.
  • Refreshing means repeatedly updating the complete image.
  • Refresh rate is measured in Hz.
  • Progressive scan displays all lines in order; interlaced scan displays alternate line fields.

2.16 Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Do not say horizontal retrace occurs after the whole screen. It occurs after every scan line.
  • Do not say vertical retrace occurs after every row. It occurs after the complete frame.
  • Do not confuse a pixel with a scan line. A scan line contains many pixels.
  • Do not confuse refresh rate with resolution. Refresh rate is frames per second; resolution is pixel dimensions.
  • Do not say the frame buffer stores only the outline of an image. It stores pixel intensity or colour information.
  • Do not assume every modern flat-panel display contains an electron beam. The beam explanation applies specifically to CRTs.
  • Do not confuse progressive and interlaced scanning. Progressive displays lines consecutively; interlaced uses two alternate-line fields.

3Topic 3 of 6The Video ControllerYELLOW PRIORITY

Why YELLOW? The older MCA paper (CS-DE-21) has a display processor versus display controller question, which is related evidence but not a direct video-controller question.

3.1 Definition

A video controller is the hardware that controls the output of a video display. In a raster display system, it reads pixel information from the frame buffer and sends the information to the display in the required order.

3.2 Working

The video controller reads pixel values from the frame buffer one scan line at a time. It keeps track of the current screen position and moves from one pixel to the next. After completing a scan line, it proceeds to the next line. This process continues to refresh the complete display.

3.3 Important terms

  • X position: Identifies the horizontal position of a pixel.
  • Y position: Identifies the vertical position of a pixel.
  • Horizontal synchronisation: Coordinates the display of each scan line.
  • Vertical synchronisation: Coordinates the start of a new frame.

Exam answer

A video controller is a hardware unit that controls the output of a display device. It reads pixel values from the frame buffer in raster-scan order and sends them to the screen. It keeps track of the horizontal and vertical pixel positions and coordinates the display of scan lines and frames. Thus, it helps refresh the complete image on the monitor.

4Topic 4 of 6Random Scan ProcessorRED PRIORITY

Question-paper relevance: The supplied MCA 2022–24 paper asks about raster and random display systems. The older MCA paper also includes a raster-scan versus random-scan question.

4.1 Definition

A random scan display, also called a vector display, draws an image by directing the electron beam only to the parts of the screen where lines or shapes are required. It does not scan the entire screen row by row.

4.2 Random Scan Display Processor

The display processor reads drawing instructions from a display file stored in system memory. These instructions describe the lines that form the picture. The processor executes the commands one by one and directs the display to draw each line. After the last command, it returns to the first command and repeats the process to refresh the picture.

4.3 Working

  1. The application program generates graphics commands.
  2. The graphics package stores the drawing commands in a display file in system memory.
  3. The display processor reads and executes each command.
  4. The electron beam moves directly to the required screen positions and draws the lines.
  5. After completing the display file, the processor starts again from the first command to refresh the picture.

4.4 Advantages and limitations

AdvantagesLimitations
Produces smooth and sharp line drawings.Not suitable for realistic images containing many shades and filled areas.
Requires less memory for simple line drawings.Refresh rate depends on the number of lines; complex pictures take longer to refresh.

Exam answer (5 marks)

A random scan processor is used in a vector display system. The picture is stored as a set of line-drawing commands in a display file in system memory. The display processor reads these commands one by one and directs the electron beam to draw only the required lines on the screen. After executing all commands, it returns to the beginning of the display file and repeats the process to refresh the image. Random scan displays produce smooth line drawings but are not suitable for realistic images with many shades.

5Topic 5 of 6Input Devices for Operator InteractionBLACK PRIORITY

Why BLACK? No matching question was identified in the checked papers for this topic.

5.1 Definition

Input devices are hardware devices through which a user enters data, commands or graphical information into a computer. In computer graphics, they allow the operator to select objects, specify screen positions and create or modify drawings.

5.2 Types of input devices

1. Keyboard

A keyboard is used to enter text, numbers and commands. In computer graphics, it can also be used to enter coordinates, labels and command options.

2. Mouse

A mouse is a pointing device used to move the screen pointer, select objects, click commands and draw or position graphical elements.

3. Trackball

A trackball is a pointing device containing a ball that is rotated with the fingers to move the screen pointer. The device itself remains stationary.

4. Joystick

A joystick consists of a stick mounted on a base. Moving the stick in different directions controls the pointer or an object on the screen. It is used in interactive graphics and simulation applications.

5. Light Pen

A light pen is a pen-shaped pointing device used to select or draw directly on a compatible display. Its light-sensitive sensor detects the screen position.

6. Digitizer / Graphics Tablet

A digitizer converts drawings or positions made with a stylus on a tablet into digital coordinate information that the computer can process.

7. Touch Panel

A touch panel allows the operator to select commands or screen positions by touching the display surface with a finger or stylus.

8. Voice Input

A voice input system accepts spoken commands through a microphone and converts them into instructions that can be used to control graphics operations.

Exam answer (5 marks)

Input devices for operator interaction are used to enter commands, positions and graphical information into a computer. Important devices include the keyboard, mouse, trackball, joystick, light pen, digitizer, touch panel and voice input system. A keyboard enters text and commands, while pointing devices such as the mouse, trackball and joystick control screen positions. A light pen and digitizer help select or create graphical objects. Touch panels and voice systems provide additional ways to interact with graphics applications.

6Topic 6 of 6Image ScannersBLACK PRIORITY

Why BLACK? No matching question was identified in the checked papers for this topic.

6.1 Definition

An image scanner is an input device that captures photographs, drawings or documents from paper and converts them into a digital image that can be stored and edited on a computer.

6.2 Working of an Image Scanner

  1. The photograph or document is placed on the scanner.
  2. The scanner illuminates the material and captures the reflected light using its sensing system.
  3. The captured information is converted into digital data.
  4. The digital image is transferred to the computer, where it can be stored, viewed or edited.

6.3 Uses

  • Converting printed photographs and drawings into digital images.
  • Storing paper documents on a computer.
  • Editing or enhancing scanned images using graphics software.

Exam answer (5 marks)

An image scanner is an input device used to convert printed images, photographs and documents into digital form. The document is placed in the scanner and illuminated. The scanner captures the reflected light and converts the image information into digital data. This data is transferred to the computer for storage, viewing and editing. Image scanners are used for digitising photographs, drawings and paper documents.
