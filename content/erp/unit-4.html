1. Meaning of ERP

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) is an integrated information system that helps an organisation manage and coordinate its major business activities using a common database and connected applications.

ERP connects functions such as purchasing, inventory, production, sales, finance and human resources. Information entered in one business function can be made available to other authorised functions, reducing duplicate data entry and disconnected records.

Simple meaning: ERP is a shared system that helps different departments work with consistent information and coordinated processes.

Important characteristics

  • Integration: connects different departments and business processes.
  • Common database: reduces separate, inconsistent data stores.
  • Real-time or timely information: updates can be available to other functions as transactions are processed.
  • Standardised processes: supports consistent ways of recording and managing work.
  • Modular structure: organisations can use connected modules for different functions.
  • Role-based access: users access information and actions according to their permissions.

2. Manufacturing and ERP

Manufacturing is the process of converting raw materials and components into finished products through activities such as planning, production, assembly, inspection and packaging.

A manufacturing organisation must coordinate material availability, machines, employees, production schedules, product specifications, quality checks, finished-goods inventory and customer orders.

What makes this integrated?

  • Inventory provides the available stock position.
  • Production planning uses demand and material information.
  • Purchasing responds to identified shortages.
  • Production consumes materials and records output.
  • Quality, warehouse and finance receive the relevant transaction information.

Not every ERP automatically performs every decision. The system supports workflows and calculations, while authorised employees configure, review and approve activities.

7. Common Data Used by Manufacturing ERP

  • Item or material master: identifies materials, components and finished products.
  • Bill of Materials (BOM): lists the components and quantities needed to make a product.
  • Routing: describes the sequence of manufacturing operations.
  • Work centre: identifies a machine, production line or group of resources where work is performed.
  • Inventory records: show stock quantities and locations.
  • Production order: authorises and tracks the manufacture of a specified quantity.
  • Supplier and customer records: store business-partner information.

These records are often called master data or operational data depending on their purpose. Accurate data is essential: an incorrect BOM or stock balance can produce incorrect planning results.

8. Benefits and Limitations of ERP in Manufacturing

Benefits

  • Better coordination between departments
  • Improved material and inventory visibility
  • More organised production planning
  • Reduced duplicate data entry
  • Improved order tracking
  • Better cost and performance reporting
  • Improved traceability when records are maintained properly

Limitations / risks

  • High implementation and maintenance cost
  • Complex implementation and configuration
  • Employee training and change-management needs
  • Incorrect data can lead to poor plans
  • Customisation may increase complexity
  • Integration and downtime risks
  • Benefits depend on process discipline and user adoption

9. Practical Example: A Bicycle Manufacturing Company

A company receives an order for 1,000 bicycles. Each bicycle requires a frame, two wheels, a seat, a handlebar, brakes and other components.

  1. Sales enters the customer order and delivery date.
  2. Production planning reviews the required quantity and available capacity.
  3. Inventory checks component stock.
  4. Purchasing arranges components that are short.
  5. Production creates work orders and records completed bicycles.
  6. Quality management records inspection results.
  7. Finished goods are dispatched; sales and finance records are updated.

Without integration, departments might use separate spreadsheets and exchange updates manually. ERP gives them a connected way to record and coordinate the order.

1. Meaning of CAD and CAM

CAD stands for Computer-Aided Design. It refers to the use of computer software to create, modify, analyse and document the design of a product or component.

CAM stands for Computer-Aided Manufacturing. It refers to the use of computer systems and software to plan, control or support manufacturing operations, often by generating instructions for automated machines.

Simple difference: CAD helps create the product design; CAM helps use that design information to manufacture the product.

2. Computer-Aided Design (CAD)

CAD software allows engineers and designers to prepare accurate digital representations of products before they are physically manufactured.

Main functions of CAD

  • 2D drafting: creates technical drawings using lines, dimensions and annotations.
  • 3D modelling: creates three-dimensional representations of parts and assemblies.
  • Design modification: allows dimensions, shapes and features to be changed.
  • Assembly design: checks how different components fit together.
  • Visualisation: helps designers inspect the shape and appearance of a product.
  • Engineering documentation: prepares drawings and design specifications for production.
  • Design analysis support: some CAD systems connect with simulation and engineering-analysis tools.

Types of CAD representation

TypeMeaningExample use
2D CADRepresents objects using two-dimensional drawings.Floor plans and engineering drawings.
3D CADRepresents objects as three-dimensional models.Machine parts and product assemblies.

Examples of CAD software

  • AutoCAD – commonly used for 2D drafting and also supports 3D workflows.
  • SolidWorks – 3D mechanical design and product modelling.
  • Autodesk Inventor – mechanical design and 3D CAD.
  • CATIA – complex product and engineering design.

3. Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM)

CAM uses computer-based tools to prepare and manage manufacturing activities. In a common CNC workflow, CAM software uses a digital part model to generate toolpaths and machine instructions.

Main functions of CAM

  • Process planning: helps decide how a component will be manufactured.
  • Toolpath generation: calculates the path a cutting tool should follow.
  • Machine instruction generation: generates machine-readable instructions, often through post-processing into a suitable CNC format.
  • Machining simulation: helps preview operations and identify possible collisions or errors.
  • Manufacturing support: supports repeatable machining and production preparation.

Common CAM-related equipment

EquipmentUse
CNC milling machineRemoves material using rotating cutting tools.
CNC latheRotates a workpiece while a cutting tool shapes it.
CNC routerCuts or engraves materials such as wood, plastic or composites.
Industrial robotPerforms programmed tasks such as welding, painting or material handling.
Important: CAM is not the same as the physical machine. CAM software prepares manufacturing data or instructions; the machine controller executes compatible instructions.

5. Integration of CAD and CAM

CAD and CAM are more useful when design information can be transferred to manufacturing without repeatedly recreating the same geometry and dimensions.

  • CAD creates the digital product model.
  • The model or a compatible exchange file is passed to CAM.
  • CAM uses the geometry and manufacturing settings to prepare operations and toolpaths.
  • A post-processor converts toolpath information into instructions suitable for a particular machine controller.
  • The CNC machine executes the instructions to manufacture the part.

6. CAD/CAM in a Manufacturing Workflow

Consider a company producing a metal machine component.

  1. The design engineer creates a 3D model with dimensions and required features using CAD.
  2. The design is reviewed and approved.
  3. The manufacturing engineer imports the model into CAM software.
  4. The engineer selects the raw material, cutting tools, machining operations and settings.
  5. CAM generates toolpaths and machine-specific instructions through a post-processor.
  6. The machining process is simulated and checked.
  7. The CNC machine produces the component.
  8. The finished component is inspected against the drawing and specifications.

Design revisions must be controlled. If the CAD model changes, the related CAM program may need to be regenerated and checked before production.

7. Relationship Between CAD/CAM and ERP

CAD/CAM and ERP support different but connected parts of manufacturing.

  • CAD contains product design information such as geometry, dimensions and specifications.
  • CAM uses design information to prepare manufacturing operations and machine instructions.
  • ERP manages wider business information such as material purchasing, inventory, production orders, costing, sales and finance.

9. Practical Example: Designing and Manufacturing a Gear

  1. CAD: an engineer creates a 3D gear model with the required dimensions and tooth geometry.
  2. Design review: the model is checked against the engineering requirements.
  3. CAM: the manufacturing engineer selects the stock material, machine, cutting tools and machining strategy.
  4. Toolpath: CAM calculates the tool movement needed to machine the gear.
  5. Simulation: the process is reviewed for possible tool collisions or machining problems.
  6. CNC machining: the machine executes the approved program.
  7. Inspection: the gear is measured to confirm that it meets the specifications.

1. Meaning and Definition of MRP

Material Requirement Planning (MRP) is a computer-based planning technique used by manufacturing organisations to calculate what materials are required, how much is required, and when they should be available for production.

MRP starts with the production plan for finished products. It uses the Bill of Materials (BOM), current inventory records and lead times to calculate the quantities and dates of components and raw materials that must be purchased or produced.

Simple meaning: MRP makes sure that the right materials are available in the right quantity at the right time, so production is not stopped because of missing components and excess stock is avoided.

Definition for examination

Material Requirement Planning is a systematic method of determining the time-phased requirements for dependent-demand items by using the master production schedule, bill of materials and inventory status records.

2. Why is MRP Needed?

Manufacturing a product may require hundreds or thousands of components. A company must know the quantity of each component and the date by which it is needed. Manual estimation becomes difficult when demand, stock and production schedules change.

  • Complex products: A finished product may contain many subassemblies and components.
  • Dependent demand: The need for components depends on how many finished products are planned.
  • Time coordination: Materials must arrive before the operation that uses them.
  • Inventory control: Too little stock stops production; too much stock blocks money and storage space.
  • Changing schedules: MRP recalculates requirements when production plans or inventory records change.

4.1 Master Production Schedule (MPS)

The Master Production Schedule states which finished products are to be produced, in what quantities and in which time periods. It is the starting point for calculating material requirements.

Example: The MPS may state that 100 bicycles must be completed in Week 4.

4.2 Bill of Materials (BOM)

A Bill of Materials is a structured list of all raw materials, components and subassemblies needed to manufacture one unit of a finished product. It also specifies the quantity of each item.

Simple product structure / BOM

Bicycle

↓
Frame × 1

The BOM tells MRP which components and quantities are needed for each bicycle.

4.3 Inventory Status Records

These records show the current material position. They commonly include:

  • On-hand quantity: stock physically available.
  • Scheduled receipts: quantities already ordered and expected to arrive.
  • Allocated stock: stock already reserved for other orders.
  • Lead time: time required to purchase or manufacture an item.
  • Safety stock: extra stock kept as protection against uncertainty, where applicable.
Remember: MPS answers “What finished products and when?”, BOM answers “Which components and how many?”, and inventory records answer “What do we already have or expect to receive?”.

5. Working Process of MRP

  1. Read the MPS: Identify the finished products, quantities and due dates.
  2. Explode the BOM: Break each finished product into its required components and subassemblies.
  3. Check inventory: Consider available stock and scheduled receipts.
  4. Calculate net requirements: Subtract usable inventory and expected receipts from gross requirements.
  5. Apply lot-sizing rules: Decide the order quantity, such as exact requirement or a fixed batch size.
  6. Offset by lead time: Work backwards from the date the material is needed to find when the order must be released.
  7. Prepare planned orders: Generate planned purchase orders or manufacturing orders.
  8. Update the plan: Recalculate when schedules, stock or supplier dates change.

6. MRP Calculations and Simple Example

6.1 Gross requirement

Gross requirement is the total quantity of an item needed before considering available stock or scheduled receipts.

6.2 Net requirement

Net Requirement = Gross Requirement − Available Supply

For a basic calculation, available supply includes usable on-hand inventory and scheduled receipts. In a full MRP record, safety stock and allocation rules may also affect the calculation.

Example: Calculate wheel requirements

A company plans to manufacture 100 bicycles. Each bicycle needs 2 wheels. The store has 30 usable wheels, and 20 wheels are already scheduled to arrive before they are needed.

StepCalculationQuantity
1. Gross requirement100 bicycles × 2 wheels200 wheels
2. On-hand stockAvailable in store30 wheels
3. Scheduled receiptAlready ordered and due on time20 wheels
4. Available supply30 + 2050 wheels
5. Net requirement200 − 50150 wheels
Result: The company needs to arrange 150 additional wheels, assuming no safety stock, scrap allowance or other adjustments.

6.3 Lead-time offsetting

Suppose the 150 wheels must be available at the start of Week 4 and the supplier lead time is 2 weeks. The purchase order should be released in Week 2, so the wheels can arrive in Week 4.

9. Types and Development of MRP

9.1 MRP I – Material Requirement Planning

MRP I focuses mainly on calculating the quantity and timing of materials and components needed for production. It uses MPS, BOM and inventory records.

9.2 MRP II – Manufacturing Resource Planning

MRP II extends material planning to broader manufacturing resources. It can include capacity planning, machines, labour, production scheduling and financial implications. It checks whether the production plan is feasible with available resources.

9.3 MRP I vs MRP II

Important syllabus connection: MRP II is listed separately in Unit IV. Here it is introduced only to explain how MRP I developed into broader manufacturing resource planning.

10. Relationship Between MRP and ERP

MRP is a manufacturing planning method. ERP is a wider integrated business system that connects manufacturing with purchasing, inventory, finance, sales, human resources and other functions.

11. Advantages and Limitations of MRP

Advantages

  • Reduces unnecessary inventory.
  • Helps prevent material shortages.
  • Improves production scheduling.
  • Supports better purchasing decisions.
  • Shows future material requirements.
  • Improves coordination between stores, purchasing and production.

Limitations

  • Depends on accurate BOM and inventory data.
  • Changes in schedules can require frequent recalculation.
  • Does not by itself guarantee sufficient machine or labour capacity.
  • Incorrect lead times can create late or early orders.
  • Implementation and maintenance require time, skills and cost.
  • Unexpected supplier disruptions can still affect production.

12. Practical Example: Manufacturing a Bicycle

A bicycle manufacturer receives an order for 100 bicycles due in four weeks. The planner enters this requirement into the MPS. The BOM shows that each bicycle needs one frame, two wheels, one seat and one handle.

  1. MRP reads the plan for 100 bicycles.
  2. It explodes the BOM to calculate 100 frames, 200 wheels, 100 seats and 100 handles.
  3. It checks available stock and scheduled receipts for each item.
  4. It calculates the net quantity still required.
  5. It uses supplier and manufacturing lead times to calculate order release dates.
  6. It creates planned purchase orders for bought-out parts and planned production orders for internally made parts.

As a result, purchasing and production teams receive a time-based plan instead of guessing material quantities.

1. Meaning and Definition of Bill of Materials

A Bill of Materials (BOM) is a complete, structured list of all raw materials, parts, components and subassemblies required to manufacture, assemble or package one unit of a finished product. It also specifies the quantity of each item required.

A BOM describes what goes into a product. It does not primarily describe the sequence of manufacturing operations; that information is generally provided by a routing.

Simple meaning: A BOM is like a recipe for making a product. Just as a food recipe lists the ingredients and their quantities, a BOM lists the parts and quantities needed to make a product.

Definition for examination

A Bill of Materials is a hierarchical record of the materials, components and subassemblies required to produce a parent item, showing the quantity of each component needed for a specified quantity of the parent item.

2. Need and Objectives of BOM

  • Identify components: Shows every material and part required for a product.
  • Calculate quantities: Specifies how many units of each component are needed.
  • Support material planning: Provides the product structure used by MRP to calculate dependent demand.
  • Control production: Helps production teams check that all required parts are available.
  • Support purchasing: Helps purchasing identify which materials must be ordered.
  • Maintain product consistency: Gives departments a common reference for the approved product structure.
  • Estimate product cost: Material quantities and item costs can be used to calculate material cost.

3. Product Structure and BOM Levels

A BOM is usually arranged in a hierarchy. The finished product is at the top, subassemblies appear below it, and individual components or raw materials appear at lower levels.

5. Types of Bill of Materials

Different BOM formats are used for different business purposes. Common types include the following.

5.1 Engineering BOM (EBOM)

An Engineering BOM is prepared by the product design or engineering team. It represents the product as designed, often organised according to design drawings and engineering specifications.

Example: The engineering team defines the frame, brake assembly, wheels and other designed parts of a bicycle.

5.2 Manufacturing BOM (MBOM)

A Manufacturing BOM represents the components and subassemblies required to manufacture and assemble the product on the shop floor. It is organised to support production activities.

Example: The factory's MBOM includes the parts, kits and subassemblies required at the relevant production stages.

5.3 Sales BOM

A Sales BOM is used when a product is sold as a package or bundle. It connects the saleable product with the items included in that package.

Example: A computer package may include a desktop unit, keyboard, mouse and monitor.

5.4 Service BOM

A Service BOM lists the parts and assemblies relevant to servicing, repairing or maintaining a product. It helps service teams identify replacement parts.

5.5 Single-level and Multi-level BOM

BasisSingle-level BOMMulti-level BOM
StructureShows only the immediate components of a parent item.Shows components and lower-level subassemblies across multiple levels.
DetailSimple and limited.More complete product structure.
ExampleBicycle → frame, wheels, seat.Bicycle → wheel assembly → rim, spokes, tyre.
UseSimple products or quick overview.Complex products and detailed material planning.
Exam tip: Do not confuse the format (single-level or multi-level) with the business purpose (engineering, manufacturing, sales or service). They describe different ways of classifying a BOM.

6. Process of Creating and Maintaining a BOM

  1. Identify the finished product: Select the product or assembly for which the BOM is required.
  2. Collect design information: Use approved drawings, specifications and engineering documents.
  3. Identify all components: List raw materials, purchased parts and subassemblies.
  4. Define quantities: Specify the quantity of each component needed for one parent item.
  5. Arrange the hierarchy: Place components under the correct parent and create the required levels.
  6. Review and validate: Check quantities, units, part numbers and completeness.
  7. Approve and release: Make the approved BOM available to planning and production.
  8. Control revisions: Update the BOM when an approved product change occurs and maintain version history.

8. Role of BOM in MRP and ERP

The BOM is one of the three main inputs of MRP. The Master Production Schedule states how many finished products are planned, while the BOM converts that finished-product quantity into component requirements. Inventory records are then checked to calculate net requirements.

9. Advantages and Limitations of BOM

Advantages

  • Provides a complete list of product components.
  • Supports accurate material planning.
  • Helps purchasing calculate material needs.
  • Improves coordination between engineering and production.
  • Supports product costing and inventory control.
  • Helps manage product changes through revisions.

Limitations / Challenges

  • Creating a detailed BOM can take time.
  • Incorrect quantities or part numbers cause planning errors.
  • Frequent product changes require careful revision control.
  • Different departments may use inconsistent item descriptions if standards are weak.
  • A BOM alone does not specify the complete sequence or timing of manufacturing operations.

10. BOM vs Routing

BOM and routing are related manufacturing records, but they answer different questions.

BasisBill of Materials (BOM)Routing
Main questionWhat materials and components are required?How and in what sequence is the product made?
InformationParts, quantities, units and product structure.Operations, work centres, sequence and processing times.
PurposeMaterial planning and product structure definition.Production scheduling and process planning.
ExampleOne bicycle requires two wheels.Fit wheels after preparing the frame.
Remember: BOM = WHAT is needed. Routing = HOW and in what sequence it is made.
