1.1 Meaning of Product Data Management

During product development, a company creates many documents and files, such as product drawings, CAD models, specifications, part details and bills of materials. These files are used by design, engineering and manufacturing teams.

Product Data Management (PDM) is a system used to store, organize, control and share all product-related information in a centralized manner. It helps employees access the correct and latest product data.

Definition: Product Data Management is the process of managing product-related information, such as engineering drawings, CAD files, specifications, bills of materials and revision records, through a centralized system.

In simple words, PDM keeps all important product information organized in one controlled place instead of allowing different departments to maintain separate and possibly outdated copies.

1.2 Need for PDM

In a manufacturing company, product designs may be changed many times. If different departments use different versions of a drawing, mistakes can occur during purchasing or production. PDM is needed to:

  • keep product information in one central location;
  • make sure users work with the correct version of a design;
  • control who can view or change product files;
  • maintain the history of design changes; and
  • share approved product information with other departments.

1.3 Types of Data Managed by PDM

Data typeExamples
Design dataCAD drawings, 2D drawings and 3D product models.
Product specificationsDimensions, materials, weight, tolerances and technical details.
Bill of Materials (BOM)Parts, components and quantities required to make a product.
DocumentsDesign reports, manuals, test reports and instructions.
Revision and change recordsEarlier versions of files and details of approved design changes.

1.4 Main Functions of PDM

  1. Data storage: Stores product files and documents in a central repository.
  2. Document management: Organizes drawings, specifications and other product documents for easy access.
  3. Version control: Maintains different versions of a design and helps users identify the latest approved version.
  4. Access control: Gives permission to authorized users to view, edit or approve product information.
  5. Change management: Records design changes, their reasons and approval status.
  6. Search and retrieval: Helps users find product information using details such as part number, name or project.
  7. Workflow management: Sends documents for checking, review and approval before release.

1.5 Working of PDM

A PDM system generally works in the following way:

  1. The engineer creates a drawing, CAD model or product document.
  2. The file is stored in the PDM system with details such as part number and revision.
  3. Authorized users review or update the file.
  4. The system records changes and sends the file for approval.
  5. After approval, the latest version is released for use by the concerned departments.

Example: A car company changes the design of a brake component. PDM stores the new drawing as a revised version and keeps the previous record. This helps the production team use the approved drawing rather than an outdated one.

1.6 Relationship Between PDM and ERP

PDM and ERP are different systems, but they can work together. PDM mainly manages product design and engineering information, whereas ERP manages business activities such as purchasing, inventory, production planning and finance.

When both systems are integrated, approved product details and bills of materials from PDM can be shared with ERP. ERP can then use this information for material planning, purchasing and production.

BasisPDMERP
Main focusProduct design and engineering data.Business resources and operations.
InformationDrawings, CAD files, specifications and BOMs.Inventory, purchase orders, production and financial records.
PurposeControls product information and design changes.Integrates and manages business processes.
Remember: PDM manages product information, while ERP uses relevant approved information to manage business operations. PDM does not replace ERP.

1.7 Important Points for Examination

  • PDM stands for Product Data Management.
  • It stores product-related information in a centralized system.
  • It manages CAD files, drawings, specifications, BOMs and revision records.
  • Its important functions include storage, document management, version control, access control and change management.
  • PDM can be integrated with ERP to share approved product information.

2.1 Introduction to Data Management

Every organisation uses data to perform its daily activities. A company stores details of customers, suppliers, employees, products, stock, purchases and sales. If this information is incorrect, duplicated or unavailable, business work may be delayed and decisions may be wrong.

In an ERP system, different departments use common business information. Therefore, data must be collected, stored, organised, checked, protected and updated properly. This complete activity is called Data Management.

2.2 Definition of Data Management

Explanatory definition: Data Management is the systematic process of collecting, organising, storing, maintaining, protecting and using an organisation's data throughout its life cycle. In an ERP system, it ensures that departments work with accurate, consistent and up-to-date information for their business activities.

Exam-ready definition — What is Data Management?
Data Management is the process of collecting, storing, organising, maintaining, protecting and controlling data so that it remains accurate, consistent, secure and available for authorised use. In ERP, it ensures that reliable information is shared across different departments to support business operations and decision-making.

In simple words: Data Management means taking proper care of business information—from the time it is entered into the system until it is updated, used, archived or removed.

2.3 Need for Data Management in ERP

  1. Centralised information: ERP connects departments through shared information, so users can work from common approved records.
  2. Accuracy: Correct customer, supplier, product and stock details reduce mistakes.
  3. Avoiding duplication: It prevents the same customer or product being entered several times under different names.
  4. Better decisions: Managers need reliable information to plan purchases, production, sales and finance.
  5. Security: Business information must be protected from unauthorised access or changes.
  6. Easy reporting: Well-organised data helps prepare reports and check performance.

2.4 Main Types of Data in an ERP System

ERP data can be understood through these important categories. The first three are especially useful for understanding how an ERP system works.

TypeMeaningExamples
1. Master DataBasic business information used repeatedly in many activities. It usually remains useful for a long period, although it can be updated.Customer, supplier, employee, material and product details.
2. Transaction DataInformation created when a business activity or transaction takes place.Sales order, purchase order, invoice, payment, goods receipt and stock movement.
3. Organisational DataInformation describing the structure and units of an organisation.Company, plant, warehouse, sales organisation and department.
4. Reference DataStandard values used to classify or describe other data.Country codes, currency codes, units of measurement and payment terms.
Easy example: A customer record is master data. When that customer places an order, the order is transaction data. The plant handling the order is organisational data. A currency code such as INR is reference data.

2.5 Master Data and Transaction Data — Difference

Change frequencyUsually changes less often.Created frequently during daily operations.

2.6 Important Activities of Data Management

  1. Data collection: Gathering information from departments, forms, existing systems and business activities.
  2. Data entry and creation: Entering new records into ERP using approved formats and rules.
  3. Data validation: Checking whether information is complete and follows required rules. For example, the system may reject an invalid date or missing customer number.
  4. Data storage and organisation: Saving information in a structured way so authorised users and ERP modules can find it.
  5. Data updating: Changing records when details change, such as a supplier's address or a product's description.
  6. Data integration and sharing: Making relevant information available to connected departments and ERP modules.
  7. Data security: Using user permissions and other controls to prevent unauthorised viewing or modification.
  8. Backup and recovery: Keeping recoverable copies so important information can be restored after accidental loss or system failure.
  9. Archiving and disposal: Keeping old records for the required period and disposing of information safely when it is no longer needed and rules allow it.

2.7 Data Management Process in ERP

  1. Collect: Obtain business information, such as customer, supplier or product details.
  2. Enter: Add the information to ERP using the correct fields and format.
  3. Validate: Check for missing values, incorrect formats and duplicate records.
  4. Store: Save approved information in the ERP database.
  5. Share and use: Allow authorised departments to use the same information in purchasing, sales, inventory and accounting.
  6. Maintain: Update records when changes occur and monitor their accuracy.
  7. Archive or remove: Retain or dispose of old information according to company policy and legal requirements.

The process does not end after data is stored. Information must be checked and maintained as the organisation changes.

2.8 Data Quality in ERP

Data quality means how suitable and reliable information is for its intended use. Good-quality data has these characteristics:

  • Accuracy: Information correctly represents the real business detail.
  • Completeness: Required information is present.
  • Consistency: The same information does not conflict across departments.
  • Timeliness: Information is updated and available when needed.
  • Uniqueness: Unnecessary duplicate records are avoided.
  • Validity: Information follows the required format and business rules.

Example: If the same supplier is entered twice with slightly different names, purchasing may create duplicate orders or finance may maintain separate payment records. Checking and correcting such records improves data quality.

2.9 Data Management During ERP Implementation

When an organisation starts using a new ERP system, it may need to transfer information from old software, spreadsheets or paper records. This activity is called data migration.

  1. Identify which old records are needed in the new ERP.
  2. Collect and examine the existing data.
  3. Remove duplicate, incorrect or unnecessary records.
  4. Convert information into the format required by the new ERP.
  5. Map old data fields to the correct new ERP fields.
  6. Load the data into the new system.
  7. Check records and accuracy before live business work begins.
Remember: Data migration means moving data from an old system to a new system. Data management is broader: it includes the ongoing care and control of data before, during and after migration.

2.10 Advantages of Proper Data Management

  • Improved accuracy: Reduces errors caused by incomplete or incorrect records.
  • Better coordination: Departments can use common and consistent information.
  • Faster work: Employees spend less time searching for or correcting records.
  • Better decisions: Managers can rely on reports created from dependable data.
  • Improved security: Access rules help protect confidential business information.
  • Reliable reporting: Consistent data supports financial, inventory and operational reports.
  • Smoother ERP operation: Connected modules exchange information with fewer data-related problems.

2.11 Problems Caused by Poor Data Management

  • Duplicate customer, supplier or product records.
  • Incorrect stock balances and purchase requirements.
  • Errors in invoices, payments and financial reports.
  • Delays because employees cannot find correct information.
  • Unauthorised access or accidental loss of important data.
  • Wrong management decisions based on outdated or incomplete reports.

2.12 Practical Example

Consider a company that purchases raw materials from suppliers and manufactures products. Its ERP stores supplier details, material information, purchase orders, goods receipts and payments.

  • The supplier's name, address and payment terms are master data.
  • The material code, description and unit of measurement are also master data.
  • A purchase order and goods receipt are transaction data.
  • The ERP checks required fields, stores records and makes approved information available to purchasing, inventory and finance.

If the supplier's details are incorrect, the purchase order or payment may also be affected. Proper data management helps prevent these problems.

3.1 Meaning of Benefits of PDM

Explanatory definition: Benefits of Product Data Management (PDM) are the improvements an organisation gains by storing, organising, controlling and sharing product-related information through a common system. PDM helps teams use correct and updated product data, coordinate their work, reduce mistakes and complete product development more efficiently.

Exam-ready definition — What are the benefits of PDM?

The benefits of PDM are the advantages obtained by centrally managing product information, such as improved data accuracy, faster access to information, better teamwork, controlled document versions, fewer design errors, reduced costs and improved productivity.

3.2 Major Benefits of PDM

  1. Centralised storage of product data: Product information such as CAD drawings, specifications, bills of materials (BOMs) and technical documents is stored in one organised location. This reduces scattered files and duplicate records.
  2. Quick access to correct information: Employees can search and retrieve the required product data easily. They spend less time searching emails, folders and different systems.
  3. Version and revision control: PDM keeps track of different versions of drawings and documents. It helps users identify the latest approved version and prevents work based on outdated designs.
  4. Improved collaboration: Engineers, designers, production staff, purchase teams and quality teams can share the same information. This improves communication between departments and locations.
  5. Reduction in errors and rework: Using approved and updated information reduces mistakes caused by incorrect drawings, missing details or old specifications. Less rework saves time and money.
  6. Higher productivity and faster development: Easy access, reuse of existing designs and automated workflows help employees complete tasks faster and reduce product development time.
  7. Better document and change control: PDM supports approval workflows and records product changes. Relevant departments can be informed when a design or specification is updated.
  8. Improved data security: Access permissions help ensure that only authorised users can view, edit or approve sensitive product information. This protects confidential designs and intellectual property.
  9. Better product quality: Correct specifications and controlled revisions help ensure that products are designed and manufactured according to approved requirements.
  10. Improved decision-making and visibility: Managers can access reliable product information and change history, helping them plan resources and make informed decisions.

3.3 How PDM Benefits an Organisation — Example

Suppose a company manufactures electric fans. The design team updates the motor specification. With PDM, the revised drawing and specification are saved as an approved version and made available to production, purchasing and quality teams. Production avoids using the old drawing, purchasing orders the correct motor, and quality inspectors check the product against the latest specification. This reduces confusion, errors and delay.

  7. It improves document security through access control.
  8. It supports better product quality and decision-making.

4.1 Meaning of Finance Module in ERP

Explanatory definition: The Finance Module is the part of an ERP system that manages an organisation’s financial transactions and accounting activities. It brings information about income, expenses, payments, receipts, assets and budgets together so that the organisation can monitor its financial position and prepare reliable reports.

Exam-ready definition — What is the Finance Module in ERP?

The Finance Module in ERP is an integrated software component used to record, process, monitor and report an organisation’s financial transactions, including accounting, payments, receipts, budgeting, cash management and financial reporting.

4.2 Objectives of the Finance Module

  • Maintain accurate and complete financial records.
  • Automate accounting, payment and billing activities.
  • Monitor income, expenses, assets and liabilities.
  • Provide timely financial reports to management.
  • Support budgeting, planning and financial control.
  • Help the organisation meet accounting and tax requirements.

4.3 Main Components / Functions of the Finance Module

  1. General Ledger (GL): It is the main book of accounts where financial transactions are summarised under categories such as assets, liabilities, income, expenses and capital. It is the base for preparing the trial balance and financial statements.
  2. Accounts Payable (AP): It manages money the organisation has to pay to suppliers and vendors. It records supplier invoices, payment due dates, approvals and payments. Example: recording and paying a raw-material supplier’s bill.
  3. Accounts Receivable (AR): It manages money customers owe to the organisation. It handles customer invoices, receipts, outstanding balances and follow-up of overdue payments. Example: recording payment received from a customer.
  4. Cash and Bank Management: It monitors cash received and paid, bank balances, bank transactions and bank reconciliation. It helps the organisation understand available cash and plan payments.
  5. Fixed Asset Accounting: It maintains records of long-term assets such as buildings, machinery, computers and vehicles. It records asset purchase, transfer, depreciation and disposal.
  6. Budgeting and Financial Planning: It helps prepare budgets, allocate funds to departments and compare actual spending with planned amounts. This helps control unnecessary expenses.
  7. Cost and Profitability Analysis: It helps identify the cost of activities, products, departments or projects and analyse where the organisation earns profit or incurs loss.
  8. Tax and Statutory Reporting: It helps calculate and record applicable taxes and prepare financial information required for tax and regulatory reporting. Exact tax features depend on the ERP and local rules.
  9. Financial Reporting: It generates reports such as the trial balance, profit and loss statement, balance sheet and cash-flow statement to help management understand financial performance.

4.4 Integration of Finance with Other ERP Modules

The finance module is connected with other ERP modules. When a business activity takes place, the related financial information can be transferred to accounting automatically or through configured workflows.

  • Purchase / Materials Management: Supplier invoices and material purchases update payable and inventory-related accounts.
  • Sales and Distribution: Customer billing updates revenue and accounts receivable.
  • Inventory: Stock receipts, issues and valuation affect inventory-related financial records.
  • Human Resources / Payroll: Salary and employee expense information can be posted to finance.
  • Production: Material, labour and overhead costs help calculate product and production costs.

Simple flow: Business transaction → relevant ERP module → financial posting → General Ledger → financial reports.

4.5 Benefits of the Finance Module

  • Accuracy: Reduces repeated manual entry and accounting mistakes.
  • Real-time information: Gives updated information about payments, receipts and balances.
  • Time saving: Automates routine accounting and reporting tasks.
  • Better financial control: Helps monitor budgets, expenses and outstanding payments.
  • Improved cash management: Provides visibility of cash inflows and outflows.
  • Better decisions: Reports help managers plan investments and control costs.
  • Transparency and accountability: Transaction records and approval controls make activities easier to trace.
  • Integrated operations: Connects financial data with purchasing, sales, inventory, payroll and production.

4.6 Practical Example

A manufacturing company purchases raw materials from a supplier for ₹50,000. The purchase information is recorded through the purchasing process. The Finance Module records the supplier liability in Accounts Payable and updates the relevant accounting records. When the company pays the supplier, the payment is recorded and the outstanding payable is reduced. The General Ledger reflects the accounting impact, and management can view the updated financial position.

4.7 Short Exam Answer

Q. Explain the Finance Module of ERP and its main functions.

The Finance Module is an important part of an ERP system. It manages and controls the financial activities of an organisation by recording transactions and providing financial information for decision-making.

Its main functions are:

  1. General Ledger: Maintains the main record of financial transactions.
  2. Accounts Payable: Manages payments due to suppliers.
  3. Accounts Receivable: Manages money to be collected from customers.
  4. Cash and Bank Management: Tracks cash, bank balances and payments.
  5. Fixed Asset Accounting: Records assets, depreciation and disposal.
  6. Budgeting: Helps prepare budgets and control expenses.
  7. Financial Reporting: Prepares financial statements and reports.

The Finance Module is integrated with other ERP modules such as sales, purchase, inventory, production and payroll. Therefore, it improves accuracy, saves time, strengthens financial control and supports better decision-making.

4.8 Quick Revision

  • GL = main book of accounts.
  • AP = money to be paid to suppliers.
  • AR = money to be received from customers.
  • Cash management = cash, bank and liquidity.
  • Fixed assets = asset records and depreciation.
  • Budgeting = planned spending and control.
  • Reporting = financial statements for decisions.

5.1 Introduction to Plant Maintenance

Every manufacturing organisation uses machines, tools, equipment and other physical assets to produce goods. These machines must be kept in good working condition. Due to continuous use, machines may develop faults, wear out or stop working. Regular inspection, servicing and repair are therefore necessary.

Plant Maintenance is the activity of inspecting, servicing, repairing and maintaining machines, equipment and other physical assets of an organisation so that they remain safe, reliable and available for production.

Exam-ready definition — What is Plant Maintenance?

Plant Maintenance is the process of inspecting, maintaining and repairing plant machinery, equipment and other technical assets to keep them in proper working condition, prevent unexpected breakdowns and ensure continuous and efficient production.

In simple words: Plant Maintenance means taking proper care of factory machines and equipment through regular checking, servicing and repair so that production work is not interrupted.

5.2 Objectives of Plant Maintenance

  1. Prevent machine breakdown: Identify possible problems and take action before machines stop working.
  2. Increase equipment life: Regular servicing and timely repair help machines remain useful for a longer period.
  3. Reduce production downtime: Keep machines available so that production can continue with fewer interruptions.
  4. Maintain product quality: Properly working machines help produce goods with consistent quality.
  5. Improve safety: Inspection and repair reduce risks to workers and equipment.
  6. Control maintenance cost: Planned maintenance can reduce expensive emergency repairs and production losses.
  7. Use resources properly: Plan the required technicians, spare parts, tools and maintenance time.

5.3 Types of Plant Maintenance

Plant Maintenance can be performed in different ways depending on the condition of the equipment and the maintenance plan.

TypeMeaningExample
1. Preventive MaintenanceMaintenance performed at planned time intervals or according to usage, before a breakdown occurs.Changing machine oil every three months and checking belts every month.
2. Corrective MaintenanceMaintenance carried out to correct a fault or defect that has been identified in equipment.Replacing a damaged bearing after inspection detects a problem.
3. Breakdown / Reactive MaintenanceRepair performed after equipment has stopped working or failed.Repairing a motor after it suddenly stops during production.
4. Predictive MaintenanceMaintenance planned using condition-monitoring information to estimate when a failure may occur.Using vibration or temperature readings to identify early signs of motor failure.
Remember the difference: Preventive maintenance is done before failure according to a schedule. Corrective maintenance fixes an identified defect. Breakdown maintenance responds after a failure. Predictive maintenance uses equipment-condition information to decide when maintenance is needed.

5.4 Main Activities / Functions of Plant Maintenance

  1. Inspection: Checking machines and equipment to identify their actual condition, wear, damage or faults.
  2. Preventive servicing: Performing regular activities such as cleaning, lubrication, tightening and replacement of parts according to a schedule.
  3. Repair: Restoring faulty or damaged equipment to proper working condition.
  4. Maintenance planning: Deciding what work is required, when it will be done and how much time it will take.
  5. Resource and spare-parts management: Arranging technicians, tools, materials and spare parts required for maintenance work.
  6. Work-order management: Recording maintenance work, assigning responsibility and tracking the progress of the job.
  7. Maintenance records: Maintaining equipment details, repair history, service dates, work performed and maintenance costs.
  8. Performance monitoring: Reviewing breakdowns, downtime and maintenance expenses to improve future maintenance activities.

5.5 Plant Maintenance Module in ERP

In an ERP system, the Plant Maintenance Module helps an organisation manage maintenance activities in a planned and organised way. It stores equipment information, records maintenance requests, creates maintenance plans and work orders, and tracks the completion and cost of maintenance work.

It provides common information to maintenance staff and other departments. For example, when a machine needs a spare part, the maintenance process can be connected with the inventory and purchasing functions of ERP.

5.6 Working Process of Plant Maintenance in ERP

A typical maintenance process in an ERP system follows these steps:

  1. Equipment registration: Machine and equipment details are recorded in the system.
  2. Inspection or maintenance request: A user reports a fault, or a planned maintenance date becomes due.
  3. Notification: The problem or maintenance requirement is documented.
  4. Planning and work order: The maintenance team decides the work to be performed, assigns technicians and identifies required tools and spare parts.
  5. Scheduling: A suitable date and time are selected, considering machine availability and production requirements.
  6. Execution: Technicians inspect, service or repair the equipment.
  7. Completion and recording: The work order is completed and details of labour, materials, time and cost are recorded.
  8. Maintenance history: The records are saved for future inspection, analysis and planning.
How to read the diagram: Follow the steps left to right. A reported fault or a due preventive-maintenance date starts the process. The work order drives scheduling and execution, and the recorded history helps plan future maintenance.

5.7 Integration of Plant Maintenance with Other ERP Modules

Plant Maintenance is connected with other ERP modules because maintenance work requires materials, employees, production schedules and financial information.

  • Materials Management: Provides spare parts and other materials required for maintenance. If a required item is unavailable, purchasing may be initiated.
  • Production Planning: Coordinates machine maintenance with production schedules and planned shutdowns.
  • Human Resource Management: Provides information about technicians, employee availability, skills and working hours.
  • Finance / Controlling: Records maintenance expenses, labour costs, spare-parts costs and the total cost of maintenance.
  • Quality Management: Equipment condition and maintenance records can support quality checks because machine performance affects product quality.

5.8 Benefits of Plant Maintenance

  1. Reduces machine breakdowns: Regular inspection and preventive maintenance help identify problems early.
  2. Increases machine life: Proper servicing and timely repair reduce unnecessary wear and damage.
  3. Reduces downtime: Well-maintained equipment is less likely to stop unexpectedly, helping production continue.
  4. Improves productivity: Machines remain available and employees can complete production work more efficiently.
  5. Reduces operating cost: Planned work can reduce emergency repair expenses, material wastage and production losses.
  6. Improves product quality: Machines working in proper condition help maintain consistent product standards.
  7. Improves workplace safety: Inspection and repair help identify unsafe equipment conditions.
  8. Better planning and control: ERP records help managers plan maintenance schedules, spare parts, technicians and budgets.
  9. Maintains maintenance history: Records of previous faults and repairs help identify repeated problems and make better decisions.

Thus, Plant Maintenance helps an organisation keep its machines reliable, reduce interruptions, control costs and maintain efficient production.

5.9 Practical Example

Suppose a factory uses a machine to manufacture metal parts. The ERP system contains the machine's details and its regular service schedule. When the scheduled service date arrives, the system helps the maintenance team plan the work. The team checks the machine, changes its oil and replaces a worn belt. The required spare part is issued from inventory, and the technician records the completed work and time spent. The maintenance history is updated. This reduces the chance of a sudden breakdown during production.

6.1 Introduction to Quality Management

Every organisation wants to provide products and services that satisfy customer requirements. In manufacturing, a product may have defects because of poor-quality raw materials, machine problems, incorrect production methods or human errors. Therefore, an organisation must plan quality standards, inspect materials and products, identify defects and take corrective action.

Quality Management (QM) is the systematic process of planning, checking, controlling and improving the quality of materials, production processes and finished products so that they meet specified standards and customer requirements.

Exam-ready definition — What is Quality Management?

Quality Management is the process of planning, monitoring, inspecting and controlling the quality of raw materials, work-in-progress and finished goods to ensure that products meet predefined specifications, standards and customer expectations.

In simple words: Quality Management means making sure that the right quality is maintained at every stage — from purchasing raw materials to manufacturing and delivering the final product.

6.2 Objectives of Quality Management

  1. Maintain product quality: Ensure that products meet defined specifications and standards.
  2. Customer satisfaction: Deliver reliable products that fulfil customer needs.
  3. Reduce defects: Identify and prevent errors, rework and rejection.
  4. Control production processes: Monitor operations so that variations are identified early.
  5. Improve continuously: Use inspection results and defect information to improve processes.
  6. Reduce cost: Minimise wastage, scrap, rework, returns and warranty-related expenses.
  7. Ensure compliance: Maintain required quality records and follow applicable standards and procedures.
  8. Improve supplier quality: Check purchased materials and monitor supplier performance.

6.3 Main Functions of Quality Management

An ERP Quality Management module supports the following major functions:

  1. Quality Planning: Defines quality requirements, inspection plans, inspection characteristics, sampling procedures and test methods before inspection is performed.
  2. Quality Inspection: Checks raw materials, semi-finished goods, finished products and other relevant items against predefined specifications.
  3. Quality Control: Compares actual inspection results with required limits and identifies whether the product or process is acceptable.
  4. Results Recording: Stores measured values and inspection observations in the ERP system for later review and analysis.
  5. Defect Recording: Records non-conformities, defects and failures found during inspection.
  6. Corrective Action: Helps the organisation investigate the cause of a problem, assign corrective tasks and monitor their completion.
  7. Quality Certificates: Supports the preparation and management of certificates that provide information about the quality of a product or batch.
  8. Quality Reporting: Provides information and reports to evaluate quality performance, defects and inspection results.

6.4 Types of Quality Inspection

  1. Incoming / Goods Receipt Inspection: Raw materials or purchased goods are inspected when they arrive from a supplier. Example: checking the thickness and quality of steel sheets received by a factory.
  2. In-process Inspection: Materials are checked during manufacturing to find problems before the entire production process is completed. Example: checking the dimensions of a component after machining.
  3. Final Inspection: The completed product is inspected before it is transferred to finished-goods stock or delivered to a customer. Example: testing a finished electric motor before dispatch.
  4. Outgoing / Delivery Inspection: Products are checked before dispatch to ensure that the goods being delivered satisfy the required conditions.
  5. Recurring Inspection: Stock or equipment is inspected at planned intervals to confirm that it continues to meet quality requirements.
  6. Calibration / Equipment Inspection: Measuring and test equipment is checked or calibrated to ensure that its readings are reliable.

6.5 Quality Management Module in ERP

In an ERP system, Quality Management is an integrated module that connects quality-related activities with purchasing, inventory, production, sales and other business functions. It keeps quality information in a common system instead of maintaining separate records in different departments.

For example, when purchased raw material is received, the ERP system can create an inspection lot. The quality department performs the inspection, records the results and makes a decision to accept or reject the material. The result can then guide the inventory or purchasing process.

6.6 Important Components of ERP Quality Management

  • Quality Master Data: Basic information such as inspection characteristics, inspection methods, sampling procedures, catalogs and material-related quality data.
  • Inspection Planning: Inspection plans and specifications that explain what to inspect, how to inspect it and which limits or methods to use.
  • Inspection Lot: A system record/request used to carry out and document a quality inspection for a particular quantity of material, product or equipment.
  • Results Recording: Entry and evaluation of measured values or observations for the inspection characteristics.
  • Defects Recording: Documentation of faults or results that do not meet the required specifications.
  • Usage Decision: The final decision about whether the inspected material or product is accepted, rejected, blocked, sent for rework or otherwise processed according to the organisation's rules.
  • Quality Notifications: Records used to report and manage quality problems, investigate causes, assign corrective tasks and track their status.
  • Quality Information and Certificates: Reports, evaluations and certificates that help monitor quality performance and communicate product quality information.
Important exam terms: An inspection lot is the record for an inspection. Results recording stores what was found. A usage decision records the final accept/reject decision after the required inspection results are evaluated.

6.7 Working Process of Quality Management in ERP

A typical ERP quality inspection follows these steps:

  1. Define quality requirements: The organisation sets specifications, inspection characteristics and acceptable limits.
  2. Create an inspection plan: The system stores the inspection method, sampling procedure and characteristics to be checked.
  3. Trigger inspection: A business event, such as goods receipt or completion of production, creates an inspection requirement.
  4. Create inspection lot: The ERP system or authorised user creates an inspection lot for the relevant material or product.
  5. Perform inspection: The inspector checks the sample or product according to the inspection plan.
  6. Record and evaluate results: Measured values and defects are entered and compared with the defined specifications.
  7. Make usage decision: The authorised user or configured system process decides whether the material is accepted or rejected.
  8. Take follow-up action: Depending on the decision, stock may be released, blocked, returned, reworked or scrapped. A quality notification or corrective action may also be initiated.
  9. Store quality history: The system retains inspection and defect information for reporting and future improvement.

6.8 Integration of Quality Management with Other ERP Modules

  1. Materials Management (MM): QM checks purchased materials at goods receipt. Inspection results help decide whether received stock can be released for use, kept blocked or returned to the supplier.
  2. Production Planning (PP): QM supports inspection during production and after production. Defects found during manufacturing can be reported so that production processes can be corrected.
  3. Sales and Distribution (SD): QM supports quality checks before delivery and helps maintain quality-related information or certificates for customers.
  4. Plant Maintenance (PM): QM can support inspection and calibration of machines, measuring devices and technical equipment.
  5. Finance / Controlling: Quality-related costs, such as scrap, rework, inspection and non-conformance costs, can support cost monitoring and analysis.

6.9 Benefits of Quality Management in ERP

  • Improved product quality: Regular inspection helps ensure that products meet required specifications.
  • Higher customer satisfaction: Better-quality products reduce complaints and increase customer confidence.
  • Early defect detection: Problems can be identified during purchasing or production rather than after delivery.
  • Reduced wastage and cost: Less scrap, rework, rejection and product return helps reduce operating costs.
  • Centralised information: Quality plans, inspection results and defect records are available in one integrated system.
  • Better supplier control: Inspection records help an organisation evaluate the quality of materials received from suppliers.
  • Faster decisions: Recorded results and system-based workflows help authorised employees make acceptance or rejection decisions.
  • Traceability: Inspection history makes it easier to review when, how and against which specifications a product or batch was checked.
  • Continuous improvement: Reports and defect analysis help identify repeated problems and improve processes.

6.10 Practical Example

Suppose a company manufactures electric fans and purchases copper wire from a supplier. When the copper wire arrives, the ERP system creates an inspection lot. The quality inspector checks the wire's thickness, conductivity and quantity according to the inspection plan. The inspector records the measured results in the system.

  • If the results meet the required specifications, the quality department makes an accepted usage decision and the material can be released for production.
  • If the results do not meet the specifications, the material may be rejected or blocked. A quality notification can be created and the purchasing department can coordinate with the supplier.

This example shows how ERP Quality Management connects inspection with inventory, purchasing and production.
