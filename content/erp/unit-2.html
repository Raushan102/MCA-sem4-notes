1. Meaning of Information and Information Systems

What is data?

Data means raw facts that have not yet been organized or interpreted. Examples include customer ID 1042, product quantity 20, invoice amount ₹15,000 and employee attendance of 8 days.

What is information?

Information is data that has been processed, organized or interpreted so that it becomes meaningful and useful for a particular purpose. For example, “Product A has only 5 units left, while average daily sales are 10 units” is information that can help a manager decide to reorder stock.

Orders, sales, stock, employee records

What is an information system?

An information system is an organized combination of people, procedures, data and technology that collects, processes, stores and distributes information to support the activities and decisions of an organization.

ElementMeaningERP example

Remember: Data is the raw input; information is the meaningful output. An information system uses people, procedures and technology to turn data into useful information.

2. Why Integrated Management Information Is Needed

In an organization, departments are connected through business activities. A sales order can affect inventory, production, purchasing, delivery and finance. If each department keeps information separately, the organization may not have one consistent view of what is happening.

Integrated management information means that information generated or updated in one functional area can be shared with other relevant areas through a coordinated information system. In ERP, this is commonly supported by connected modules and a shared database.

Problems created by isolated information

  • Data duplication: the same customer or product details may be entered in several departments.
  • Inconsistent records: sales may show one address while accounts or delivery records show another.
  • Delayed information: departments wait for emails, phone calls or manual reports.
  • Limited visibility: a manager cannot easily see the complete status of an order.
  • Repeated work: employees enter the same information more than once.
  • Slow decisions: reports must be collected and combined from separate systems.
Core idea: An enterprise needs information to move across departmental boundaries, not remain trapped inside individual departments.

How integration works

  1. A user enters or updates business data in the appropriate ERP module.
  2. The system validates and records the transaction.
  3. Relevant information becomes available to authorized connected modules.
  4. Other departments use the updated information for their own activities.
  5. Managers can generate reports using information from multiple functions.

Important clarification: Integration does not mean every employee can see every record. Access is controlled by roles and permissions. It means authorized users and modules can access the information required for their work.

6. Characteristics and Components

Key characteristics

  1. Common data source: departments rely on coordinated, shared business records.
  2. Data entered once where practical: the same transaction details need not be repeatedly re-entered by every department.
  3. Cross-functional integration: connected modules support processes that cross departmental boundaries.
  4. Timely information: authorized users can access updated information without waiting for separate manual reports.
  5. Consistent definitions: common codes and formats help reduce conflicting records.
  6. Management reporting: information from multiple functions can be combined for analysis.
  7. Controlled access: roles and permissions help protect sensitive information.

7. Benefits and Limitations

Benefits

  • Better coordination: departments work with connected information.
  • Reduced duplicate entry: common data can be reused across modules.
  • Improved accuracy: fewer repeated manual transfers can reduce inconsistency.
  • Faster reporting: managers can combine information from different functions.
  • Better visibility: order, stock and payment status can be viewed across the process.
  • Improved decisions: timely and consistent information supports planning and control.
  • Process efficiency: connected workflows reduce unnecessary hand-offs.

Limitations and challenges

  • Implementation cost: software, infrastructure, migration and training require investment.
  • Data quality dependency: incorrect input can spread errors to connected processes.
  • Change management: employees need training and may resist new procedures.
  • Security responsibility: centralized information requires strong access control and protection.
  • Integration complexity: connecting older or external systems may require additional work.
  • Dependence on system availability: outages can affect several departments at once.

Therefore, integrated management information is valuable, but the benefits depend on correct implementation, reliable data, suitable controls and employee adoption.

8. Practical Business Example

Imagine an electronics company that sells laptops through its website and physical stores.

  1. A customer orders a laptop online. The sales module records the order.
  2. The inventory module checks stock in the nearest warehouse.
  3. If the item is available, stock is reserved for that order.
  4. The delivery team receives the dispatch details.
  5. The finance module creates the invoice and records payment status.
  6. Management can view sales, stock, pending deliveries and revenue through reports.

Without integrated information, each team might maintain a separate record and manually communicate updates. With ERP, relevant information is shared across connected modules, making the order process easier to coordinate.

1. Meaning of Business Modeling

Business modeling is the process of creating a structured representation of how an organization works. It describes the organization as one integrated system and shows its major business functions, processes, people, resources, information flows, and the relationships among them.

In simple words, business modeling is like drawing a map of a company. The map explains what the company does, who performs each activity, what information is needed, and how one activity affects another.

A business model represents the organization as one connected system

Important: A business model in this ERP context is not merely a way to earn revenue (such as B2B or B2C). It is a representation of the actual business structure and the way its functions and processes work together.

Simple example

Consider an electronics company. A customer places an order. Sales checks product availability, inventory confirms stock, the warehouse dispatches the item, and finance generates an invoice and records payment. These are not isolated activities; together they form a connected business process. Business modeling makes these connections visible.

2. Why is Business Modeling Needed?

Before implementing ERP, an organization must understand how its business actually operates. Installing software without understanding the existing processes can automate confusion rather than improve the business.

  • Understand the complete organization: provides a broad picture of departments, functions, and activities.
  • Identify relationships: shows how one department depends on another.
  • Find process gaps: exposes duplicate work, delays, unnecessary approvals, and missing information.
  • Support ERP selection and configuration: helps determine which ERP modules and workflows are required.
  • Improve coordination: clarifies the information each department must provide to others.
  • Support process improvement: helps redesign inefficient activities before or during ERP implementation.
  • Provide a basis for the data model: the business processes and information requirements guide the design of the underlying data structure.
Remember: Business modeling generally comes before configuring the ERP system. First understand and represent the business; then decide how the ERP system should support it.

Business processes are central

A business process is a sequence or group of related activities that uses people, information, and other resources to produce a defined result. A process may cross several departments.

4. Business Processes and Workflows

A workflow describes the sequence in which tasks are performed, including the order of activities, the people responsible, and the decisions or conditions that move work forward.

Common enterprise processes

  • Procure-to-pay: identify a need → request purchase → approve → order from supplier → receive goods → verify invoice → pay supplier.
  • Order-to-cash: receive customer order → check stock → pick and dispatch → issue invoice → receive payment.
  • Plan-to-produce: forecast demand → plan production → check materials → manufacture → inspect finished goods.
  • Record-to-report: record transactions → post accounts → reconcile → prepare financial reports.

These processes connect departments. For example, a sales order can affect inventory, warehouse operations, delivery planning, and accounting.

6. Relationship Between Business Modeling and ERP

ERP software is designed to support and integrate business processes. Business modeling helps an organization explain those processes before deciding how to configure the ERP system.

How business modeling supports ERP implementation
Understand business goals
→
Map current processes
↓
Identify gaps and requirements
→
Design / improve processes
↓
Create business and data requirements
→
Configure ERP modules
↓
Test and refine
→
Operate and monitor

Current-state model (AS-IS): documents how work is performed today.

Future-state model (TO-BE): describes how work should operate after improvement and ERP implementation.

Comparing AS-IS and TO-BE helps identify what must change in processes, roles, controls, and systems. ERP should support the agreed business process; it should not be configured blindly.

Standardization and customization

During ERP implementation, an organization may adapt its process to standard ERP practices or customize the software to preserve a special process. Excessive customization can increase cost and complexity, so the business model helps the organization decide which differences are genuinely necessary.

8. Benefits and Limitations

Benefits

  • Gives a common understanding of how the organization operates.
  • Improves communication between departments.
  • Helps identify bottlenecks, duplicated tasks, and unnecessary steps.
  • Provides requirements for ERP configuration and data design.
  • Supports process standardization and continuous improvement.
  • Clarifies responsibilities and information needs.

Limitations / challenges

  • A model may become outdated when business processes change.
  • Incomplete input from employees can produce an inaccurate model.
  • Very complex diagrams may be difficult to understand.
  • Departments may disagree about the best process or ownership.
  • A model alone does not improve operations; the organization must implement and monitor the changes.

1. Meaning of Integrated Data Model

An integrated data model is a unified representation of the data used throughout an organization. It describes the important business entities, their attributes, and the relationships among them, so that different departments can work with consistent and connected information.

In simple words, it is a common blueprint that explains what information the company keeps and how different pieces of information are related. Sales, inventory, purchasing, production, and finance may use the same business information instead of maintaining disconnected versions of it.

Important distinction: An integrated data model is the logical blueprint of enterprise data. It is not simply a drawing of one database, nor does the term always require every byte of data to be physically stored in one database. Modern architectures can provide an integrated view across connected data stores as well.

2. Why Does ERP Need an Integrated Data Model?

Before ERP, departments may maintain separate files or databases. The same customer or product can be recorded more than once, sometimes with different spellings, codes, addresses, or balances. This creates inconsistency and makes organization-wide reporting difficult.

Without integrationWith an integrated data model
Each department may maintain its own copy of customer information.Departments use a consistent customer definition and shared records.
Stock figures may differ between sales and warehouse systems.Stock information is connected to the same product and inventory concepts.
Information must be re-entered between systems.Data captured in one process can be reused by other authorized processes.
Reports may disagree because definitions differ.Common definitions improve consistency in reporting.

ERP requires information integration and process automation. Its data model should represent the organization as a whole rather than only one department.

Remember: The purpose is not merely to collect more data. It is to make enterprise data consistent, related, reusable, and available to authorized business functions.

3. How Does an Integrated Data Model Work?

  1. Identify business entities: determine the important things about which the organization stores information, such as Customer, Supplier, Product, Employee, Order, and Invoice.
  2. Define attributes: specify the details recorded for each entity. For example, a Customer may have Customer_ID, Name, Address, and Contact_Number.
  3. Define relationships: explain how entities connect. For example, one customer can place many orders.
  4. Use common definitions: departments agree on meanings, identifiers, formats, and rules.
  5. Connect ERP processes and modules: transactions entered through one process can be used by other connected modules, subject to access permissions.
  6. Maintain data quality: validation, ownership, and controlled updates help keep information accurate.

The business model describes how the organization works. The integrated data model describes the information needed to represent that business. The database implements the data structures, while ERP programs allow users to perform tasks with that information.

5. Main Components of an Integrated Data Model

ComponentMeaningExample
EntityA business object or concept about which data is stored.Customer, Product, Supplier.
AttributeA property or detail of an entity.Customer_Name, Product_Price.
RelationshipA connection between entities.Customer places Order.
Identifier / KeyA field used to identify a record or link records.Customer_ID, Order_ID.

Three levels of data modeling

  • Conceptual data model: high-level view of the main business entities and their relationships; focuses on what the business needs to represent.
  • Logical data model: adds more detail such as attributes, keys, and relationship rules without tying the model to a particular database product.
  • Physical data model: describes how the design is implemented in a specific database, such as tables, columns, data types, indexes, and constraints.
Memory trick: Conceptual = what things exist. Logical = how they are structured and related. Physical = how the database stores them.

6. Characteristics of a Good Integrated Data Model

  • Enterprise-wide: represents data required across the organization, not just one isolated department.
  • Consistent: uses common definitions, formats, and identifiers.
  • Connected: shows meaningful relationships among business entities.
  • Minimizes unnecessary duplication: avoids maintaining conflicting copies of the same business facts where practical.
  • Supports business processes: provides information needed for transactions and process automation.
  • Accurate and governed: defines ownership, validation, and update responsibilities.
  • Secure: allows users to access information according to their roles and responsibilities.
  • Adaptable: can accommodate legitimate changes in business requirements.

7. Benefits of an Integrated Data Model

  1. Reduced data redundancy: helps avoid unnecessary repeated storage of the same business information.
  2. Improved data consistency: shared definitions reduce conflicting values across departments.
  3. Better information sharing: authorized employees can access information relevant to their work.
  4. Timely information: connected processes can use updated transaction information without waiting for manual consolidation.
  5. Improved reporting: common data definitions make organization-wide reports more dependable.
  6. Process automation: data captured in one step can support later steps in a business process.
  7. Better coordination: departments can work with connected information rather than isolated records.
  8. Supports decision-making: managers can obtain a more complete view of business operations.

8. Challenges and Precautions

ChallengeExplanationGood practice
Poor data qualityIncorrect or incomplete legacy data can enter the new system.Clean, validate, and deduplicate data before migration.
Different definitionsDepartments may use the same word with different meanings.Agree on a data dictionary and common standards.
Legacy system integrationOlder systems may use different formats and identifiers.Map fields and define controlled transformation rules.
Access and privacyNot every employee should see every record.Apply role-based access and audit controls.
Complexity and changeBusiness requirements and structures evolve.Govern changes and maintain model documentation.

Important caution: An integrated model does not mean every employee can view or edit every item of data. Integration and access control are separate concerns. Information should be shared with authorized users according to business need.

9. Integrated Data Model vs Integrated Database

BasisIntegrated Data ModelIntegrated Database
MeaningA logical description or blueprint of enterprise data and its relationships.A database implementation that stores and manages connected data.
FocusEntities, attributes, relationships, definitions, and rules.Actual storage, retrieval, transactions, and database operations.
LevelDesign / representation.Implementation / technology.
ExampleCustomer places many Orders.Customer and Order tables linked by Customer_ID.

Many ERP systems use a common database shared by integrated modules. However, the broader idea of an integrated enterprise data model is about a consistent view and structure of enterprise information; in modern environments, connected data may also be integrated across multiple systems or stores.
