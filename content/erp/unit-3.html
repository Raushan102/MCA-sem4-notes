1. Meaning and Definition of BPR

Business Process Reengineering (BPR) is the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of an organization's business processes to achieve major improvements in important performance measures such as cost, quality, service and speed.

In simple words, BPR means asking: “If we were designing this work today, knowing what we know now, how should we perform it?” Instead of making small changes to an inefficient process, an organization may redesign the complete process from beginning to end.

Remember: BPR is not merely computerizing an old process. It is redesigning the process itself; technology such as ERP can then support the improved process.

What is a business process?

A business process is a connected set of activities that takes inputs and produces a defined result for a customer or another part of the organization.

Customer order / data
→
Activities

Delivered order

2. Why is BPR Needed?

Over time, organizations often add approvals, forms, hand-offs and separate systems to existing workflows. These additions may make work slow, costly and difficult to coordinate. BPR is considered when the existing process cannot meet business requirements.

  • Long processing time: Work waits between departments or approval stages.
  • High operating cost: Repeated work, paper handling and unnecessary checks consume resources.
  • Duplicate data entry: The same information is entered into multiple systems, increasing errors.
  • Poor customer service: Customers face delays and receive inconsistent updates.
  • Departmental silos: Teams optimize their own tasks but the complete end-to-end process performs poorly.
  • Changing competition and technology: Old procedures may not support new customer expectations or business models.

5. Relationship Between BPR and ERP

BPR and ERP are related but they are not the same thing. BPR focuses on redesigning business processes. ERP is an integrated software system that supports business activities using shared information and connected modules.

  • BPR examines how work should be performed across the organization.
  • ERP provides the integrated applications and data needed to support those workflows.
  • BPR can help an organization identify inefficient legacy practices before configuring ERP.
  • ERP implementation may also reveal opportunities for further process redesign.

Important distinction: BPR does not automatically mean purchasing ERP software. An organization may redesign a process without implementing a new ERP system. Similarly, installing ERP without examining inefficient processes may simply automate existing problems.

6. Practical Example: Processing a Customer Order

Before BPR — traditional process

  1. Sales receives an order and records it in a spreadsheet.
  2. Sales emails the warehouse to check stock.
  3. Warehouse replies later, sometimes after manually checking records.
  4. Finance separately verifies the customer's payment status.
  5. Sales prepares another document for dispatch.
  6. Customer receives an update only after several manual follow-ups.

Problems: repeated data entry, waiting between departments, delayed decisions and limited visibility.

After BPR — redesigned process supported by ERP

  1. Sales enters the order once into the ERP system.
  2. The system makes order information available to relevant departments.
  3. Inventory availability and customer credit status can be checked using shared data and configured business rules.
  4. Once required checks are satisfied, fulfilment and invoicing activities proceed through the redesigned workflow.
  5. Authorized staff and the customer can receive timely status information.

Result sought: fewer hand-offs, less duplicate entry, faster processing and better visibility. Actual results depend on process design, data quality, system configuration and employee adoption.

10. Quick Self-Check

1. Is BPR only the installation of new software?

No. BPR redesigns business processes. Software may support the redesigned process.

2. What is the difference between AS-IS and TO-BE?

AS-IS describes the current process; TO-BE describes the desired redesigned process.

3. Why should BPR involve multiple departments?

Because an end-to-end process often crosses departmental boundaries. Cross-functional involvement helps reveal hand-offs and design a coordinated workflow.

4. Does every BPR project require ERP?

No. BPR can be performed independently. ERP is one possible technology platform for supporting redesigned processes.

1. Meaning and Definition of MIS

MIS stands for Management Information System. It is an organized combination of people, procedures, data and technology that collects and processes data and supplies useful information to managers for planning, controlling, coordinating and decision-making.

In simple language, an MIS converts business data into meaningful reports so that managers can understand what is happening in an organization and decide what action should be taken.

Exam-ready definition: A Management Information System is an integrated system that collects, processes, stores and distributes relevant information to support the operations, management and decision-making functions of an organization.

Understand the three words

  • Management: Planning work, organizing resources, directing employees and controlling performance.
  • Information: Processed and organized data that is useful for a particular purpose or decision.
  • System: Interrelated components working together to achieve a common objective.

Thus, MIS is not merely a computer or a software application. It includes the people who use information, the procedures followed, the data collected and the technology used to process and communicate it.

Remember: Data is the input; information is the useful output. MIS mainly transforms organizational data into information that managers can use.

3. Objectives and Functions of MIS

The main objective of MIS is to provide the right information to the right person, in the right form, at the right time, so that organizational work and decisions can be improved.

Main objectives

  1. Support decision-making: Supply facts and reports that help managers choose an appropriate course of action.
  2. Improve planning: Provide past and current information for setting targets, preparing budgets and allocating resources.
  3. Support control: Compare actual performance with plans and highlight deviations.
  4. Improve coordination: Share consistent information among departments such as finance, sales, production and HR.
  5. Increase efficiency: Reduce repetitive manual reporting and unnecessary duplication of data.
  6. Provide timely reports: Make relevant information available when managers need it.
  7. Maintain information: Store organizational records in an organized and retrievable form.
  8. Monitor performance: Help managers track sales, costs, inventory, productivity and other measures.

Functions of MIS

  • Collect data from internal and, where required, external sources.
  • Validate, classify, calculate, summarize and store data.
  • Generate routine, periodic, exception and on-demand reports.
  • Distribute information to authorized users.
  • Provide feedback that helps managers monitor and improve operations.

5. How MIS Works

MIS generally follows an input–processing–storage–output–feedback cycle.

INPUT
Sales, purchases, stock, payroll data
↓
PROCESSING
Validate, classify, calculate and summarize
↓
DATABASE / STORAGE
Organized business records
↓
OUTPUT
Reports, summaries, alerts and dashboards
↓
MANAGERIAL ACTION
Plan, decide and control
↺ Feedback and updated data

Step-by-step explanation

  1. Input: Data enters from business activities, forms, transactions, sensors or other systems.
  2. Processing: The system checks, sorts, calculates and summarizes the data.
  3. Storage: Records are stored so they can be retrieved and used later.
  4. Output: The system produces information such as a monthly sales report or low-stock report.
  5. Decision and action: A manager studies the report and takes action.
  6. Feedback: The result of the action and new business transactions become fresh data for the system.

9. Practical Example: MIS in an ERP-Based Retail Business

Imagine a retail company with several stores. Each store records sales through its billing system, and inventory changes when products are sold or received.

  1. A cashier records a sale. This transaction is captured by the operational transaction system.
  2. The ERP system updates sales and inventory records using shared business data.
  3. MIS prepares a daily or weekly report showing sales by store, product and region.
  4. The regional manager notices that one product is selling quickly in one store but slowly in another.
  5. The manager reviews the report and decides to transfer stock or change the next purchase quantity.
  6. New sales and stock movements update the system, allowing the next report to reflect the change.
Why this example matters: It shows the complete connection: business transaction → integrated ERP data → MIS report → managerial decision → updated business activity.

11. Benefits and Limitations of MIS

Benefits

  • Improves the quality and speed of managerial decisions.
  • Provides a common view of organizational performance.
  • Supports planning, budgeting and control.
  • Improves coordination and communication between departments.
  • Helps identify unusual performance, delays and resource shortages.
  • Reduces duplicate reporting and manual effort when well designed.
  • Maintains historical information for comparisons and trend analysis.

Limitations and challenges

  • Incorrect input: Poor-quality data can produce misleading reports.
  • Cost: Development, software, hardware, training and maintenance can be expensive.
  • Security and privacy: Sensitive organizational data must be protected.
  • Resistance to change: Employees may resist new procedures or systems.
  • Overdependence: Managers may rely too heavily on reports and ignore context or professional judgment.
  • Inflexibility: A poorly designed system may not adapt to changing business requirements.
  • Information overload: Too many reports can make important information harder to identify.

1. Meaning and Definition of DSS

DSS stands for Decision Support System. It is an interactive computer-based information system that helps managers analyse data, compare alternatives and make decisions, especially when a problem is complex, changing or not fully structured.

In simple language, DSS helps a manager answer questions such as: What may happen if prices are reduced? Which supplier is the best choice? How much stock should we maintain? Which business option gives the best result?

Exam-ready definition: A Decision Support System is an interactive, computer-based system that uses data and analytical models to support managerial decision-making in semi-structured and unstructured situations.
Important: DSS supports the decision-maker; it does not automatically replace the manager's judgement or take responsibility for the final decision.

Understand the words

  • Decision: Selecting one course of action from two or more alternatives.
  • Support: Providing relevant data, analysis, comparisons and possible outcomes.
  • System: A coordinated combination of data, software, models, procedures and users.

2. Why is DSS Needed?

Managers regularly face decisions where information is incomplete, many factors are involved, or the result of each option is uncertain. A routine report may show what happened, but a manager may also need to explore what could happen under different conditions.

  • Business environments change quickly.
  • Managers must compare several alternatives.
  • Some decisions are new and do not have a fixed procedure.
  • Large volumes of data are difficult to analyse manually.
  • Managers need to understand the likely impact of changing price, demand, cost, capacity or supply.

3. Types of Managerial Decisions

A common way to understand DSS is to classify decisions according to how clearly the problem and solution procedure are defined.

Decision typeMeaningExampleTypical support
StructuredRoutine and repetitive; clear rules or procedures exist.Reordering stock when it reaches a fixed reorder level.TPS and predefined rules; DSS may assist with analysis.

4. Characteristics and Capabilities of DSS

  1. Interactive: Users can ask questions, change inputs and explore results.
  2. Flexible: It can support different problems and changing assumptions.
  3. Data-driven: It uses relevant internal and external data.
  4. Model-driven: It applies mathematical, statistical, financial or simulation models.
  5. Supports multiple alternatives: It compares possible courses of action.
  6. What-if analysis: It shows how results may change when assumptions change.
  7. Timely support: It helps managers analyse information when a decision is required.
  8. User-oriented: Results are presented through reports, tables, charts or dashboards.
  9. Human judgement: The manager interprets results and makes the final decision.

What-if analysis: a simple example

A company currently sells 1,000 units at ₹500 each. A manager wants to test what may happen if the selling price is reduced to ₹475 and expected sales increase to 1,100 units. A DSS can calculate and compare the revenue under both assumptions.

ScenarioUnitsPriceRevenue
Current1,000₹500₹5,00,000
Proposed1,100₹475₹5,22,500

The proposed scenario gives ₹22,500 more revenue in this simplified example. However, revenue is not profit: the manager must also consider costs, capacity, customer response and other factors.

1. Database and Database Management System (DBMS)

  • Stores data required for analysis.
  • Data may come from internal sources such as sales, inventory and finance, and external sources such as market or supplier information.
  • DBMS helps store, retrieve, organise and manage the data.

2. Model Base and Model Base Management System (MBMS)

  • Contains models used to analyse a decision problem.
  • Examples include forecasting, optimisation, statistical, financial and simulation models.
  • MBMS helps select, store and use the appropriate model.

3. Dialogue Generation and Management System (DGMS) / User Interface

  • Provides the communication link between the user and DSS.
  • Allows the manager to enter assumptions, select models and request analysis.
  • Displays results using reports, tables, charts and dashboards.
Exam tip: If asked for the traditional three components, write Database + Model Base + Dialogue/User Interface. Explain the function of each and draw the diagram.

6. How Does DSS Work?

  1. Identify the problem: The manager defines the decision that must be made.
  2. Collect data: Relevant internal and external data are gathered.
  3. Select a model: A suitable analytical model is chosen.
  4. Enter assumptions: The user provides values or constraints to test.
  5. Analyse alternatives: DSS calculates or simulates possible outcomes.
  6. Compare results: Options are evaluated using relevant criteria.
  7. Make the decision: The manager selects an action.
  8. Review the outcome: Actual results can be compared with expectations to improve future decisions.

8. Models and Analytical Techniques Used in DSS

  • What-if analysis: Changes input values to see how an outcome may change.
  • Sensitivity analysis: Examines how strongly the result responds to changes in one or more variables.
  • Goal-seeking analysis: Starts with a desired result and works backwards to find the input needed.
  • Optimisation: Finds the best solution under stated objectives and constraints.
  • Simulation: Imitates a real process to study possible outcomes under different conditions.
  • Forecasting: Estimates future values using historical data and suitable methods.
Difference to remember: What-if asks “What happens if I change this input?” Goal-seeking asks “What input is required to reach this target?” Sensitivity analysis measures how much the output changes as assumptions vary.

12. Benefits and Limitations of DSS

Benefits

  • Improves the quality and consistency of decisions.
  • Helps compare alternatives systematically.
  • Supports faster analysis of complex problems.
  • Allows what-if, sensitivity and scenario analysis.
  • Improves use of organisational data.
  • Helps managers understand risks and possible outcomes.
  • Supports planning, forecasting and resource allocation.

Limitations

  • Data quality: Incorrect or incomplete input can produce misleading results.
  • Model limitations: A model simplifies reality and may omit important factors.
  • Cost: Development, software, infrastructure and maintenance can be expensive.
  • Skills: Users may need training to interpret outputs correctly.
  • Over-reliance: Managers may trust system recommendations without questioning assumptions.
  • Security: Sensitive business data must be protected.
  • Uncertainty: DSS cannot guarantee that a prediction or selected option will succeed.

1. Meaning and Definition of EIS

EIS stands for Executive Information System. It is a computer-based information system designed to provide senior executives with quick access to important, summarised and often visual information about the organisation and its business environment.

In simple language, EIS gives top-level managers a high-level view of how the organisation is performing. It helps them monitor key results, identify trends, notice unusual situations and focus on issues that may require strategic attention.

Exam-ready definition: An Executive Information System (EIS) is a computer-based system that provides senior executives with timely, summarised and relevant internal and external information to support strategic planning, monitoring and decision-making.
Remember: EIS supports executives; it does not make strategic decisions on their behalf. The executive interprets the information and decides what action to take.

Understand the words

  • Executive: Senior manager or top-level decision-maker, such as a CEO, director or managing director.
  • Information: Processed and meaningful facts, such as revenue trends, profit margins and market changes.
  • System: Connected technology, data, software and procedures that deliver the required information.

2. Why is EIS Needed?

Senior executives are responsible for the overall direction and performance of an organisation. They cannot examine every transaction or detailed operational report. They need a concise view of important indicators and the ability to investigate an issue when necessary.

  • Executives have limited time and need information in a compact form.
  • Business information is spread across departments and systems.
  • Management must monitor targets, performance and major risks.
  • External factors such as competitors, customer demand and regulations can affect strategy.
  • Executives need to identify trends, exceptions and emerging problems early.
  • Strategic decisions often require information from several business functions.

3. Characteristics of EIS

  1. Executive-oriented: Designed around the information needs of senior management.
  2. Easy to use: Presents information through simple screens, dashboards and visual reports.
  3. Summarised information: Highlights key indicators instead of displaying every transaction.
  4. Drill-down capability: Allows an executive to move from a summary to more detailed information when required.
  5. Trend analysis: Helps compare performance across periods and identify patterns.
  6. Exception reporting: Highlights results that cross defined limits or differ from expected performance.
  7. Timely access: Provides regularly refreshed or near-real-time information, depending on the underlying data systems.
  8. Internal and external information: May combine organisational data with market, economic or competitor information.
  9. Visual presentation: Uses charts, graphs, scorecards and dashboards to make patterns easier to understand.

4. Objectives and Functions of EIS

1. Meaning and Definition of OLAP

OLAP stands for Online Analytical Processing. It is a technology used to analyse large amounts of business data from different perspectives. It helps users examine trends, compare performance and discover patterns for decision-making.

In simple language, OLAP lets a manager ask analytical questions about business data, such as: Which product generated the highest sales? Which region performed best? How did sales change from one quarter to another?

Exam-ready definition: Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) is a data-analysis approach that enables users to perform multidimensional analysis of large volumes of data quickly to support reporting, trend analysis and business decision-making.
Remember: OLAP is mainly for analysis. It is different from systems that record day-to-day transactions, such as order entry and billing systems.

2. Why is OLAP Needed?

  • Organisations generate large amounts of data through sales, finance, inventory, production and customer activities.
  • Managers need to analyse data across dimensions such as time, product, region and customer.
  • Ordinary transaction reports may not easily answer complex comparative questions.
  • OLAP helps identify trends, exceptions and relationships in historical data.
  • It supports planning, performance evaluation and informed business decisions.

3. Characteristics of OLAP

  1. Multidimensional view: Data can be analysed across several dimensions, such as product, location and time.
  2. Fast analytical queries: Designed to retrieve summaries and comparisons efficiently.
  3. Historical analysis: Often works with accumulated data over months or years.
  4. Interactive analysis: Users can change dimensions and levels of detail to explore data.
  5. Aggregation: Combines detailed values into totals, averages and other summaries.
  6. Complex calculations: Supports comparisons, ratios, growth rates and other analytical measures.
  7. Read-intensive use: Typically performs many analytical reads rather than frequent transaction updates.

An OLAP cube is a way of organising data for analysis across multiple dimensions. Despite the name, a cube can contain more than three dimensions.

For example, a retail company may analyse sales using three dimensions: Time, Product and Region. The numerical value being analysed is called a measure, such as sales amount or quantity sold.

5. Main OLAP Operations

OLAP operations allow users to view the same data in different ways and at different levels of detail.

5.1 Roll-up

Roll-up summarises data by moving from a lower level of detail to a higher level in a hierarchy. Example: daily sales are combined into monthly sales, then quarterly sales.

Daily sales → Monthly sales → Quarterly sales → Yearly sales
More detail ───────────────→ More summary

5.2 Drill-down

Drill-down is the opposite of roll-up. It moves from summarised data to more detailed data. Example: yearly sales → quarterly sales → monthly sales → daily sales.

Yearly sales → Quarterly sales → Monthly sales → Daily sales
Summary ─────────────────→ More detail

5.3 Slice

Slice selects one value of a dimension to create a smaller view. Example: show sales only for the year 2026, while retaining product and region analysis.

5.4 Dice

Dice selects a range or set of values from multiple dimensions. Example: analyse laptops and phones, in Jharkhand and Bihar, during January to March.

5.5 Pivot (Rotate)

Pivot changes the orientation of a report to view the same data from another arrangement. Example: swap rows and columns so that regions appear as columns and products as rows.

6. Types of OLAP

6.1 MOLAP – Multidimensional OLAP

MOLAP stores or organises data in multidimensional structures, often using precomputed summaries. It can provide fast query responses for supported analyses, but cube processing and storage can require additional resources.

6.2 ROLAP – Relational OLAP

ROLAP uses relational databases and tables to store data. Analytical queries are translated into database queries, commonly SQL. It can work with large volumes of relational data, though query performance depends on database design and optimisation.

6.3 HOLAP – Hybrid OLAP

HOLAP combines features of MOLAP and ROLAP. It may keep summarised data in multidimensional structures and detailed data in relational tables, aiming to balance query speed and scalability.

Managers and analysts
Reports • Trends • Business decisions
Do not confuse them: ERP is primarily used to integrate and run business processes; a data warehouse organises data for analysis; OLAP provides analytical operations over that data. They can work together but are not interchangeable terms.

10. Practical Business Example

Imagine a retail company that sells laptops and mobile phones in Ranchi, Patna and Kolkata. Management wants to compare sales by product, region and quarter.

QuestionOLAP operation / analysis
What were total sales in 2026?Roll-up across time
Show only sales in RanchiSlice by region
Compare laptops and phones in two cities during Q1Dice across product, region and time
Find which month caused a quarterly declineDrill-down from quarter to month
Display regions as columns instead of rowsPivot the report

By exploring these views, managers can identify which products and locations are contributing to growth or decline. OLAP presents the analysis; managers interpret the findings and decide what to do.

11. Benefits and Limitations of OLAP

Benefits

  • Supports multidimensional analysis of business data.
  • Makes comparison across time, products and regions easier.
  • Helps identify trends, patterns and unusual results.
  • Provides interactive analysis through roll-up, drill-down, slice, dice and pivot.
  • Supports planning, budgeting and performance evaluation.
  • Can reduce repeated manual preparation of analytical reports.

Limitations

  • Implementation cost: Data warehouse, OLAP tools and infrastructure may be expensive.
  • Data preparation: Integration, cleansing and loading data require time and effort.
  • Data quality: Incorrect or inconsistent source data can lead to misleading analysis.
  • Complexity: Cube design, dimensions and measures require careful planning.
  • Maintenance: Data and analytical structures must be refreshed and maintained.
  • Not transaction processing: OLAP is not intended to replace operational transaction systems.

1. Meaning and Definition of SCM

Supply Chain Management (SCM) is the coordination and management of all activities involved in sourcing raw materials, converting them into finished products, storing them, and delivering them to customers.

It manages the movement of products, information and money among suppliers, manufacturers, warehouses, distributors, retailers and customers.

Simple meaning: SCM makes sure that the right product reaches the right customer, in the right quantity, at the right place and time, while controlling the total cost.

Why the word “management” matters

A supply chain is not just transportation. SCM plans and coordinates the whole network so that one organisation's decisions do not create unnecessary cost or delay for another.

2. What is a Supply Chain?

A supply chain is the connected network of organisations, people, activities, resources and technology involved in making and delivering a product or service.

Important: A supply chain can be short or long. A service organisation also has a supply chain, even if its output is not a physical product.

3. Need and Objectives of SCM

  • Reduce total cost: control purchasing, production, storage and transportation costs.
  • Improve customer service: deliver orders accurately and on time.
  • Maintain suitable inventory: avoid both stockouts and excessive stock.
  • Coordinate partners: share reliable plans and information across organisations.
  • Improve responsiveness: react faster to changes in demand or supply.
  • Improve quality: manage supplier quality and production consistency.
  • Increase visibility: understand where orders, materials and products are in the chain.
Exam point: The central objective is to satisfy customer demand efficiently while optimising the performance and total cost of the entire supply chain—not merely one department.

4. Main Activities and Process of SCM

A commonly used way to understand SCM is through five connected activities: Plan, Source, Make, Deliver and Return.

SCM process cycle

3. MAKE

4. DELIVER

5. Three Important Flows in SCM

Supply chains coordinate three major flows. These flows move in different directions and must be connected.

Material / Product flowSupplier → Manufacturer → Distributor → Customer
Information flowCustomer demand, forecasts and order status move across partners
Financial flowInvoices, payments and credit generally move back through the chain
FlowMeaningExample
Material flowMovement of raw materials, components, work-in-progress and finished goods.Components shipped to a factory.

Remember: Poor information sharing can cause overproduction, excess inventory or shortages—even when transportation and factories work properly.

6. Relationship Between SCM and ERP

ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) integrates information and processes within an organisation. SCM coordinates supply-chain activities across the organisation and its external partners.

ERP supporting internal operations and SCM coordination

ERP system inside the organisation
PurchasingInventoryProductionFinanceSales
↕ Shared operational information
SCM network

Suppliers ↔ Manufacturer ↔ Warehouses ↔ Distributors ↔ Customers

How ERP supports SCM

  • Purchasing data helps procurement teams manage supplier orders.
  • Inventory records show available stock and material requirements.
  • Production plans help estimate when materials are required.
  • Sales orders and forecasts provide information about customer demand.
  • Finance records support supplier invoices, payments and cost analysis.

ERP and SCM are related but not identical. ERP commonly provides integrated internal business data; SCM extends coordination to the wider supply network. Modern ERP suites may include SCM capabilities or connect to dedicated SCM applications.

8. Benefits and Challenges of SCM

Benefits

  • Lower operating and inventory costs
  • Better customer satisfaction
  • Faster order fulfilment
  • Improved coordination
  • Better demand visibility
  • Reduced waste and delays
  • Improved supplier relationships

Challenges

  • Demand uncertainty
  • Supplier delays or failures
  • Transport disruption
  • Data quality and system integration
  • Cybersecurity and data sharing risks
  • Global regulations and complexity
  • Balancing cost with resilience

What is the bullwhip effect?

The bullwhip effect occurs when a small change in customer demand creates increasingly large fluctuations in orders as information moves upstream from retailer to distributor, manufacturer and supplier.

Illustration of increasing order variation
Customer
small change→Retailer
larger order change→Distributor
greater change→Factory / Supplier
largest variation

Ways to reduce it: share actual sales data, improve forecasting, shorten replenishment lead times, reduce unnecessary bulk ordering and coordinate plans with partners.

9. Practical Example: Supply Chain of a Smartphone

  1. Planning: the company forecasts how many phones customers may buy.
  2. Sourcing: it purchases displays, batteries, chips and other components from suppliers.
  3. Making: the factory assembles, tests and packages the phones.
  4. Delivering: finished phones move to warehouses, distributors, stores or directly to customers.
  5. Returning: defective or unwanted phones are collected for repair, replacement, refurbishment or recycling.

ERP can provide internal purchasing, inventory, production, sales and finance information. SCM processes coordinate these activities with suppliers, logistics partners and sales channels.

10. SCM vs Logistics

BasisSupply Chain ManagementLogistics
ScopeBroad; coordinates the complete supply network.More focused on movement and storage of goods.
ActivitiesPlanning, sourcing, making, delivering, returning and partner coordination.Transportation, warehousing, handling, packaging and distribution.
FocusEnd-to-end supply-chain performance.Efficient flow and storage of products.
RelationshipIncludes logistics as an important part.One major function within SCM.
One-line answer: Logistics is a part of SCM; SCM manages the broader network from suppliers to final customers.
