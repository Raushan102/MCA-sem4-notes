1.1 What is ERP?

ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) is an integrated software system that helps an organization manage and coordinate its major business activities using shared information.

It can connect departments and functions such as:

  • Finance and accounting
  • Human Resources (HR)
  • Production and manufacturing
  • Sales and marketing
  • Inventory and warehouse management
  • Purchasing and procurement

Simple example: When a mobile-phone company receives an order, ERP can connect sales, inventory, production, purchasing and finance. The departments can use consistent information instead of maintaining disconnected records.

1.2 What is the evolution of ERP?

Evolution of ERP means the gradual development of business-management systems from simple inventory-control tools to integrated software that supports an entire organization and its business partners.

ERP did not appear all at once. As organizations grew, they needed systems that could plan more resources, connect more departments and share information faster. Each stage broadened the scope of the previous approach.

1.3 Diagram — stages in the evolution of ERP

The scope expands from stock control to connected, enterprise-wide business management. The dates are approximate.

1.4 Detailed stages in the evolution of ERP

Stage 1 — Inventory Management and Control (1960s)

  • This was the earliest computer-supported stage. Organizations used computers to record raw materials and finished goods.
  • It helped track stock and maintain appropriate inventory levels, often using reorder points.
  • Main focus: inventory management — “How much stock do we have, and when should we reorder?”
  • Limitation: it mainly tracked stock; it did not plan all the components and resources required to make a product.

Stage 2 — Material Requirements Planning (MRP, 1970s)

  • MRP stands for Material Requirements Planning. It was developed to plan materials needed for production.
  • It calculates how much raw material and how many components are required, and when they should be purchased or produced.
  • It uses information such as the production schedule, the product’s Bill of Materials (BOM) and current inventory.
  • Main focus: material and production planning — “What materials are needed, in what quantity, and when?”
  • Contribution: better material availability and less unnecessary inventory.

Stage 3 — Manufacturing Resource Planning (MRP II, 1980s)

  • MRP II stands for Manufacturing Resource Planning. It expanded MRP beyond materials.
  • It considered machines, manpower, production capacity and financial planning, helping coordinate manufacturing activities.
  • It could help assess whether the planned production schedule was feasible with available resources.
  • Main focus: complete manufacturing resources — “Can we make the planned products with our available capacity, people and money?”

Stage 4 — Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP, 1990s)

  • ERP expanded the planning approach from manufacturing to the whole organization.
  • It integrated areas such as finance, HR, sales, purchasing, inventory and production.
  • Departments could share information through connected modules and a common database, improving coordination and decision-making.
  • Main focus: integration of all departments — “How can the organization manage its activities as one connected enterprise?”

Stage 5 — Extended ERP / ERP II (2000s)

  • ERP expanded beyond an organization’s internal departments and connected it with suppliers, customers and business partners.
  • Internet and web-based technologies supported external business integration.
  • ERP II could connect with Supply Chain Management (SCM), Customer Relationship Management (CRM), partner portals and e-commerce.
  • Main focus: external business integration — sharing suitable information and coordinating with the wider business network.

Stage 6 — Modern ERP / Cloud ERP (2010s to the present)

  • Modern ERP systems use internet technologies and may be delivered through cloud computing or as a subscription service.
  • Authorized users can access business information from different locations and devices.
  • They can provide timely reporting and may include analytics, workflow automation and AI-assisted features.
  • Main focus: cloud computing and intelligent business management — helping organizations use connected information, automation and analysis.

Note: Cloud ERP is a common modern option, but not every organization uses cloud ERP. On-premises and hybrid systems are also used.

1.5 Difference between the stages

StageApprox. periodMain purpose / focus

1.6 Example — ERP in a mobile-phone company

Suppose a company manufactures mobile phones. It has departments for purchasing, production, sales, finance and HR. ERP connects these departments so they can work with shared business information. The evolution sequence can be understood through the same company:

  1. Inventory Management: records the number of screens, batteries and finished phones in stock.
  2. MRP: calculates how many screens, batteries and other components are needed to meet the production plan, and when to order them.
  3. MRP II: checks whether the factory has enough machines, workers, capacity and budget for the plan.
  4. ERP: connects purchasing, production, sales, finance, inventory and HR through one integrated system.
  5. ERP II: connects the company with component suppliers, distributors and customers through web-based services.
  6. Modern / Cloud ERP: gives authorized staff access to current reports remotely and may help analyze data or automate routine workflows.

1.7 Conclusion

The evolution of ERP began with simple inventory-management systems and gradually expanded to materials planning, manufacturing resources, the whole enterprise and external business partners. Modern ERP can add cloud access, analytics and automation. This progression helps organizations coordinate activities, improve information sharing and make better decisions.

1.8 Exam-ready definition

Evolution of ERP is the gradual development of business-management systems from inventory control to MRP, MRP II, enterprise-wide ERP, extended ERP and modern cloud-based ERP. At each stage, the system’s scope grew to manage more resources, integrate more functions and support wider business coordination.

THE CENTRAL IDEA

ERP brings the important business functions of an organization into an integrated information system. Instead of each department keeping disconnected records, departments can work with connected processes and shared business information.

2.1 Meaning and Full Form of ERP

YELLOW ERP stands for Enterprise Resource Planning.

It is an integrated software system used by an organization to manage and coordinate its major business functions—such as finance, human resources, purchasing, inventory, production and sales—through connected applications and shared information.

WordMeaning in simple languageBusiness example
EnterpriseThe whole organization or business.A manufacturing company with finance, HR, sales and production departments.
ResourceAnything the organization uses to run its work.Money, employees, raw materials, machines, stock and information.
PlanningOrganizing and using resources according to business requirements.Planning how much material, labour and money are needed for an order.

2.2 Formal Definition for Examination

IMPORTANT DEFINITION

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) is an integrated, real-time, cross-functional information system that supports the major internal business processes of an organization by connecting different functional departments and enabling the flow and sharing of information.

In simple words: ERP is one connected business system through which different departments can work with the same updated organizational information.

2.3 Why Does an Organization Need ERP?

Consider a company that sells and manufactures laptops. It has separate departments for sales, inventory, production and finance.

When these departments use unrelated software, the same information may have to be entered repeatedly. A sales employee may not know the latest stock position, and the finance team may receive order details late.

Conceptual illustration of siloed departmental applications.

ERP addresses this problem by connecting the departments through a common system.

Conceptual ERP integration: departments exchange information through connected modules.

2.4 How ERP Works — A Simple Business Example

Suppose a customer places an order for 100 laptops. The order triggers connected activities in different departments.

  1. Sales: Records the customer's order in the ERP system.
  2. Inventory: Checks the available laptop stock.
  3. Production / Procurement: If stock is insufficient, the organization can plan production or purchase the required materials.
  4. Finance: Uses order and delivery information for billing and accounting.
  5. Management: Can review updated order, stock and financial information through reports.
IMPORTANT POINT

The data is entered once where appropriate and then made available to other authorized functions through integrated processes. ERP does not mean every employee can see every record; access is controlled by roles and permissions.

2.5 Major Characteristics of ERP

8. Security and access controlControls access according to user roles and responsibilities.An HR user can access employee records while a sales user accesses order data.

An ERP package is generally divided into modules. Each module supports a particular functional area, but the modules are connected to the overall ERP system.

ModuleWork performedTypical information

2.7 Basic ERP Architecture

At a conceptual level, an ERP system can be understood through three major layers. The exact technical architecture differs by product, but this model helps explain how users, business processing and data work together.

Simplified three-layer view of ERP architecture.

  1. Presentation layer: The interface through which employees enter information and view reports.
  2. Application layer: Processes business rules, validations and workflows, such as order approval or stock updates.
  3. Database layer: Stores and manages business data used by the connected modules.

Example: A sales employee enters an order using the presentation layer. The application layer validates the order and coordinates the workflow. The database layer stores the order and related information.

2.8 ERP vs Traditional / Legacy / Siloed Systems

A legacy system is an older information system that an organization continues to use. A siloed system is an application or database that works separately from other departments' systems. A legacy system can be integrated, but many older departmental systems are isolated.

BasisTraditional / siloed systemsIntegrated ERP
System structureSeparate applications for separate departments.Connected modules within an integrated system.
Data storageMay use separate departmental databases.Uses a common or integrated data repository.
Data sharingMay require manual transfer or custom interfaces.Information flows through connected processes.
Data duplicationGreater risk of duplicate entry and inconsistent records.Reduces unnecessary duplication through shared records.
ReportingReports may be limited to one department.Can combine information from multiple functions.
CoordinationDepartments may coordinate through email, files or manual updates.Connected workflows support cross-department coordination.
Exam point: Do not write that every legacy system is necessarily disconnected or bad. The important distinction is between isolated departmental applications and an integrated ERP environment.

2.9 Advantages and Limitations — Brief Overview

The official syllabus has a separate topic on advantages of ERP, so the benefits are only introduced here.

  • Benefits: Better coordination, reduced duplicate data entry, improved visibility, more consistent information and faster reporting.
  • Limitations: High implementation cost, complexity, training requirements, change resistance and dependence on data quality.

3.1 Introduction

The ERP market grew because organizations needed a better way to manage their expanding and interconnected business activities. Separate applications and outdated systems often made it difficult to share accurate information, coordinate departments, respond quickly to customers and control costs.

CORE IDEA

Organizations adopted ERP to integrate business processes, improve operational performance, obtain reliable information for decisions, modernize old systems and support business growth.

3.2 Major Reasons for the Growth of ERP

IMPORTANT

1. Improved business performance

ERP connects activities such as purchasing, inventory, production, sales and finance. Better coordination reduces unnecessary work and helps the organization complete business processes more efficiently.

Example: When a sales order is entered, inventory information can be checked without separately calling the store department.

IMPORTANT

2. Reduction in lead time and cycle time

Lead time is the total time between starting a request or order and completing it. Cycle time is the time required to complete a particular process or activity. ERP can reduce delays by connecting departments and automating information flow.

Example: A purchase request can move to approval and purchasing without repeatedly entering the same details.

IMPORTANT

3. Better customer service and satisfaction

ERP makes relevant information about orders, stock, delivery and customer accounts easier to access. Employees can respond to customer questions more accurately and help avoid unnecessary delays.

Example: A customer asks when an item will be delivered. Staff can check order and inventory status from connected information.

IMPORTANT

4. Support for business growth and expansion

As a business adds new products, customers, branches or locations, its processes become more complex. ERP provides an integrated system that can help coordinate these activities across the organization.

Example: A company opening a new branch can use common processes and shared reporting rather than maintaining completely separate records.

IMPORTANT

5. Elimination of limitations in legacy systems

Legacy systems are older applications that may be difficult to maintain, modify or connect with modern software. ERP adoption helps organizations replace disconnected or outdated applications with an integrated platform.

Example: If finance and inventory use incompatible software, employees may need to enter the same transaction twice. Integrated ERP reduces this duplication.

IMPORTANT

6. Better information accuracy and availability

When connected departments use shared business data, duplicate records and inconsistent information can be reduced. Managers can access more up-to-date information for monitoring and planning.

Example: Sales and inventory teams can refer to the same stock information instead of maintaining different spreadsheets.

IMPORTANT

7. Improved planning and decision-making

ERP brings information from different functions together. Managers can analyze sales, costs, inventory and production information to make better-informed decisions.

Example: A manager can compare sales demand with available stock and production capacity before planning the next production cycle.

IMPORTANT

8. Better utilization of resources

ERP helps organizations plan and monitor the use of materials, employees, machines, money and time. Better visibility can reduce waste and improve coordination.

Example: Production planning can use inventory information to avoid ordering materials that are already available in the warehouse.

IMPORTANT

9. Flexibility and ability to respond to change

Organizations face changing customer requirements, competition, products and business processes. Integrated information and coordinated workflows make it easier to understand the impact of changes and respond across departments.

Example: When demand for a product increases, sales, inventory and production teams can coordinate their response using connected information.

IMPORTANT

10. Advances in information technology

Developments such as client-server computing, relational databases, web-based applications, cloud computing and mobile access have made integrated enterprise systems more practical and accessible to organizations.

Example: Cloud-based ERP can allow authorized employees in different locations to access the same system through the internet.

IMPORTANT

11. Better coordination with suppliers and business partners

ERP can improve the flow of information between purchasing, inventory, production and supplier-related activities. This supports more coordinated ordering and supply planning.

Example: Purchasing staff can use stock and production requirements to plan when raw materials should be ordered.

IMPORTANT

12. Competitive advantage

Organizations use ERP to improve speed, coordination, service and the quality of management information. These improvements can help a business compete more effectively.

Example: A business that can process orders accurately and respond quickly may provide a better customer experience than a competitor using disconnected manual processes.

3.3 Tangible and Intangible Reasons

Reasons supporting ERP adoption can also be grouped into tangible and intangible benefits. Tangible benefits are easier to measure in numbers, while intangible benefits are valuable but harder to measure directly.

TypeMeaningExamples
TangibleBenefits that can usually be measured using time, cost, quantity or financial figures.Reduced lead time, lower operating cost, fewer repeated entries, improved inventory control, faster order processing.
IntangibleBenefits that are difficult to measure directly but improve organizational capability.Customer satisfaction, better flexibility, improved decision-making, stronger coordination and information quality.

The two groups describe different kinds of value that encourage organizations to invest in ERP.

3.4 Diagram — Overall Drivers of ERP Market Growth

Business needs, operational improvement and technology changes collectively increase demand for ERP solutions.

3.5 Practical Example

EXAMPLE: A GROWING MANUFACTURING COMPANY

A manufacturer initially manages sales, inventory, purchasing and accounts through separate spreadsheets and applications. As orders increase, staff face duplicate entries, delayed stock updates and difficulty preparing reports.

  1. The company needs to process orders faster and reduce delays.
  2. It wants accurate stock information and better production planning.
  3. It needs managers to see current sales, cost and inventory information.
  4. It plans to open another branch and needs common processes.
  5. It replaces disconnected applications with an ERP system.

Result: ERP can connect the departments and support coordinated work. The actual improvement depends on correct implementation, reliable data and employee adoption.

  • Integration, automation, reporting and cost control.

4.1 Introduction

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) integrates important business functions such as finance, sales, purchasing, inventory, production and human resources. Because departments can work with connected processes and shared information, an organization can improve coordination, control and decision-making.

CORE IDEA

The main advantage of ERP is that it connects business processes and information in one integrated system. This can reduce delays and duplication, improve visibility and help the organization use its resources more effectively.

4.2 Major Advantages of ERP

IMPORTANT

1. Reduction of Lead Time

Lead time is the total time between placing or initiating an order/request and completing or delivering it. ERP connects activities such as order processing, stock checking, purchasing and production planning. This can reduce waiting and communication delays.

Example: When a customer order is entered, the sales team can check stock information directly instead of waiting for a separate inventory report.

IMPORTANT

2. Reduction of Cycle Time

Cycle time is the time required to complete a particular process or activity. ERP can streamline workflows and reduce repeated data entry, manual hand-offs and unnecessary approvals.

Example: A purchase request can move through a defined approval workflow, reducing the time spent sending forms between departments.

IMPORTANT

3. On-Time Shipment

ERP connects order details with inventory, production and distribution information. This helps an organization plan material availability and delivery activities more effectively, increasing the possibility of shipping products on the promised date.

Example: The dispatch team can use order and stock status to plan which customer orders are ready to ship.

IMPORTANT

4. Improved Resource Utilization

ERP helps managers monitor and plan the use of resources such as materials, machines, employees, money and time. Better visibility can reduce idle resources, unnecessary purchases and waste.

Example: Production planning can check available raw materials before creating a new purchase order.

IMPORTANT

5. Better Customer Satisfaction

ERP can provide employees with updated information about customer orders, product availability, delivery and accounts. This supports more accurate responses and better service.

Example: Customer support can check order progress and provide a more reliable delivery update.

IMPORTANT

6. Improved Supplier Performance

ERP connects purchasing, inventory and production requirements. This helps an organization plan purchases, track supplier-related transactions and evaluate whether materials arrive as required.

Example: Purchase records can help the company review supplier delivery dates and identify repeated delays.

IMPORTANT

7. Increased Flexibility

Integrated information and standardized workflows help an organization respond to changes in demand, products, processes and business conditions. Modular ERP systems may also allow organizations to add functions as needs grow.

Example: When demand increases, managers can review sales, inventory and production information together before adjusting the production plan.

IMPORTANT

8. Reduced Quality Costs

ERP can support quality management by connecting production records, inspection results, materials and supplier information. This helps organizations identify defects and trace where quality problems may have occurred. It can reduce costs related to rework, scrap and avoidable defects when quality processes are properly managed.

Example: If a batch fails inspection, the organization can trace its production and material records to investigate the cause.

IMPORTANT

9. Improved Information Accuracy

When departments use shared data, the same transaction does not need to be entered into multiple disconnected systems. This reduces duplicate records and conflicting information. Accuracy still depends on correct data entry and proper controls.

Example: A stock update recorded in the inventory module can be reflected in information used by sales and purchasing teams.

IMPORTANT

10. Better Decision-Making Capability

ERP brings information from different departments together and provides reports, dashboards and analysis. Managers can use this information to plan operations, monitor performance and make informed decisions.

Example: A manager can compare sales, expenses and inventory levels before deciding whether to increase production.

IMPORTANT

11. Better Integration and Coordination

ERP links business functions and supports the flow of information between them. This reduces departmental information silos and helps employees coordinate work.

Example: A sales order can trigger inventory checking and provide information needed by production and finance.

IMPORTANT

12. Reduced Duplication and Manual Work

ERP automates repetitive workflows and allows multiple departments to use shared records. This can reduce repeated data entry, paperwork and errors caused by maintaining separate records.

Example: Employee details maintained in the HR module can be used by payroll instead of entering the same details again.

IMPORTANT

13. Improved Reporting and Transparency

ERP can produce reports from connected operational and financial data. Managers can get a clearer view of organizational activities, compare performance and identify issues more quickly.

Example: A finance manager can review sales and expense information from a common reporting system.

IMPORTANT

14. Better Cost Control

By streamlining processes, reducing repeated work and improving resource planning, ERP can help an organization control operating costs. The actual savings depend on the quality of implementation and how the system is used.

Example: Improved stock visibility can help prevent unnecessary over-ordering and excess inventory.

4.7 Important Note — ERP Advantages Are Not Automatic

ERP software does not guarantee that every organization will immediately reduce costs or improve service. Benefits depend on:

5.1 Introduction

ERP implementation is a complex process because it affects many departments, employees, business processes and information systems. An ERP project may fail when the organization cannot implement the system properly, employees do not adopt it, or the final system does not meet the expected business requirements.

CORE IDEA

ERP failure can happen because of poor management, weak planning, lack of user support, insufficient training, inaccurate data, excessive customization, poor testing or inadequate technical support.

5.2 What Does ERP Failure Mean?

ERP failure does not always mean that the software completely stops working. A project may also be considered unsuccessful when it:

  • Exceeds the approved budget by a large amount.
  • Takes much longer than the planned implementation time.
  • Does not provide the required business functions.
  • Creates serious disruption to normal business operations.
  • Is not accepted or properly used by employees.
  • Fails to deliver the expected benefits or is eventually abandoned.

Different studies use different definitions of “failure”. Therefore, avoid memorizing a single failure percentage unless a specific source and definition are given.

5.3 Major Reasons for ERP Failure

IMPORTANT

1. Lack of Top Management Support

ERP implementation requires decisions, funding, coordination and organizational changes. If senior management does not actively support the project, departments may not cooperate and important issues may remain unresolved.

Example: Department managers refuse to provide staff for ERP testing because senior management has not made the project a priority.

IMPORTANT

2. Poor Planning and Project Management

An ERP project needs a clear plan, defined responsibilities, milestones, risk management and regular monitoring. Poor project management can cause delays, confusion, budget overruns and incomplete implementation.

Example: The company starts installing ERP without deciding which departments and processes will be included in the first phase.

IMPORTANT

3. Unrealistic Time and Cost Estimates

ERP implementation may require software configuration, data migration, integration, testing and employee training. If the organization sets an unrealistic deadline or budget, teams may skip important activities to meet the target.

Example: Management insists on launching the entire ERP system in a few weeks even though data migration and user testing are incomplete.

IMPORTANT

4. Lack of User Involvement

Employees who actually use ERP understand many day-to-day business requirements. If they are not involved in requirement gathering, design and testing, the system may not match their real work needs.

Example: The warehouse team is not consulted, so the inventory module does not support its actual stock-handling process.

IMPORTANT

5. Resistance to Change

ERP often changes existing workflows and responsibilities. Employees may resist the new system because they are comfortable with old methods, fear losing control or do not understand the benefits of the change.

Example: Employees continue maintaining separate spreadsheets instead of entering transactions into ERP.

IMPORTANT

6. Inadequate Training and Education

ERP contains multiple modules and workflows. Without sufficient training, employees may enter incorrect data, avoid using the system or make operational mistakes.

Example: A purchase employee does not know how to create a purchase order and enters the transaction incorrectly.

IMPORTANT

7. Inaccurate Data and Data Migration Problems

Data migration means transferring data from old systems into the new ERP system. If old records are incomplete, duplicated, inconsistent or incorrectly mapped, the new system may produce unreliable results.

Example: The old inventory system contains incorrect product codes. These codes are transferred without cleaning, causing stock reports to become inaccurate.

IMPORTANT

8. Excessive Customization

Customization means modifying standard ERP software to match an organization's special requirements. Too much customization increases cost and complexity, makes upgrades harder and can create maintenance problems.

Example: The company modifies many standard ERP functions to copy every detail of its old system instead of reviewing whether the old process should change.

IMPORTANT

9. Wrong ERP Package or Vendor Selection

If the selected ERP product does not match the organization's size, industry, business processes or technical requirements, the organization may face expensive modifications and functionality gaps. Weak vendor support can make these problems harder to resolve.

Example: A manufacturing company selects a system that lacks important production-planning functions.

IMPORTANT

10. Failure to Redesign Business Processes

Business Process Reengineering (BPR) means examining and redesigning business processes to improve performance. If an organization simply copies inefficient old processes into the ERP system, it may automate existing problems instead of improving the business.

Example: A company keeps unnecessary approval steps in its new ERP workflow, so purchase requests remain slow.

IMPORTANT

11. Poor Communication and Coordination

ERP affects many departments. If information is not communicated properly between management, users, consultants and technical teams, requirements may be misunderstood and decisions may conflict.

Example: Finance and sales teams follow different definitions for order status, leading to inconsistent reports.

IMPORTANT

12. Inadequate Testing

Testing checks whether ERP functions correctly and supports real business scenarios. If testing is incomplete, errors may appear after go-live and disrupt normal operations.

Example: The company tests normal sales orders but does not test returns, cancellations or peak-volume transactions.

IMPORTANT

13. Insufficient Resources and Skills

ERP implementation needs suitable project members, business analysts, technical experts, time and budget. If the team lacks the required skills or members are not available, implementation quality may suffer.

Example: The organization assigns ERP implementation as an extra duty to already busy employees who cannot give enough time to the project.

IMPORTANT

14. Poor Integration with Existing Systems

ERP may need to exchange information with older applications, external platforms, machines or third-party services. Weak integration can create duplicate data, broken workflows and inconsistent information.

Example: The ERP system does not correctly exchange order information with the organization's online store.

IMPORTANT

15. Inadequate Post-Implementation Support

ERP work does not end at go-live. The organization needs monitoring, issue resolution, maintenance, user support and continuous improvement. Without these activities, problems may continue and employees may lose confidence in the system.

Example: After launch, employees report errors but there is no support team to resolve them quickly.

5.7 Practical Example

EXAMPLE: A MANUFACTURING COMPANY'S FAILED ERP PROJECT

A manufacturing company decides to implement ERP to connect purchasing, inventory, production, sales and finance.

  1. Management selects a very short implementation deadline.
  2. Warehouse and production employees are not involved in requirement gathering.
  3. Old stock records contain duplicate product codes, but the data is migrated without cleaning.
  4. Employees receive very little training and continue using spreadsheets.
  5. The company skips realistic testing to meet the launch date.
  6. After go-live, orders and stock reports do not match, causing operational delays.

Why did it fail? The project had poor planning, weak user involvement, inaccurate data, insufficient training and inadequate testing. The example shows how several small failures can combine into a major ERP problem.
