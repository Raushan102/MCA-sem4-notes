1Topic 1 of 6In-house ERP Implementation: Pros and Cons

YELLOW PRIORITY — Predicted / syllabus-based

Question evidence: The supplied Ranchi priority sheet does not identify a direct question specifically asking for the advantages and disadvantages of in-house ERP implementation. It is an official syllabus topic and is suitable for a 5- or 10–15-mark answer.

1.1 Introduction

ERP implementation is the process of planning, configuring, testing and introducing an ERP system in an organisation. In an in-house implementation, the organisation uses its own employees and internal project team to carry out most of the implementation activities instead of handing the complete responsibility to an outside implementation partner.

Internal employees may include IT staff, department representatives, project managers, database administrators and selected end-users. The organisation may still purchase the ERP software from an external vendor and may obtain limited specialist assistance when required.

Important clarification: In-house implementation does not necessarily mean that the company develops an ERP package from zero. It usually means that the company takes primary responsibility for implementing and configuring a purchased ERP system using its own people.

1.2 Definition

Exam-ready definition: In-house ERP implementation is an approach in which an organisation implements an ERP system mainly through its own internal employees and resources, taking responsibility for project planning, process analysis, configuration, data preparation, testing, training and ongoing support.

1.3 How in-house implementation works

  1. Form an internal project team: Select a project manager, IT staff and representatives from departments such as finance, HR, purchasing, sales and production.
  2. Study business requirements: Document existing processes, problems and the organisation's needs.
  3. Plan the ERP project: Define scope, schedule, budget, responsibilities and risks.
  4. Configure the ERP package: Set up modules, workflows, user roles, approval rules and reports using internal expertise.
  5. Prepare and migrate data: Clean existing records and transfer approved data into the new system.
  6. Test the system: Perform module, integration, security and user-acceptance testing.
  7. Train employees and go live: Train users, introduce the system and monitor early operations.
  8. Provide support: Resolve issues, maintain the system and improve processes.

1.4 Advantages of in-house implementation

  1. Better knowledge of business processes: Internal employees understand the organisation's actual work, rules, culture and operational problems.
  2. Greater control: Management can directly control project decisions, priorities, schedules and implementation methods.
  3. Improved ownership and commitment: Employees who help configure the system are more likely to understand and accept the new working environment.
  4. Knowledge retention: ERP knowledge remains within the organisation, helping with future maintenance, upgrades and improvements.
  5. Potential reduction in consulting fees: The organisation may reduce the amount of paid external consulting support.
  6. Better alignment with internal requirements: Internal staff can explain organisation-specific needs during configuration and testing.
  7. Direct communication: Department representatives can communicate issues directly to the project team.

1.5 Disadvantages of in-house implementation

  1. Lack of specialised expertise: Employees may not have sufficient experience with ERP configuration, integration, data migration or project management.
  2. Longer implementation time: Internal employees must balance ERP work with their normal duties, which can delay the project.
  3. High training requirement: Staff may need extensive training before they can configure and manage the ERP system effectively.
  4. Risk of mistakes: Incorrect configuration, weak testing or poor data migration can create serious operational problems.
  5. Limited outside perspective: Employees may continue old processes instead of identifying better practices available in the ERP package.
  6. Dependence on key employees: If trained staff leave the organisation, important system knowledge may be lost.
  7. Hidden internal cost: Employee time, training, infrastructure and project delays can make the approach expensive even when consultancy fees are lower.

1.7 Example

Suppose a medium-sized manufacturing company purchases an ERP package. It forms an internal team consisting of its IT manager, accountant, purchase officer, store manager and production supervisor. These employees map the company's processes, configure the relevant modules with available expertise, test purchase and production transactions, train colleagues and coordinate go-live. If advanced database or integration work is needed, the company may hire a specialist for that limited task. This is an internally led or in-house implementation.

1.8 Conditions for success

  • Strong support from top management.
  • Availability of trained internal employees.
  • Clear project scope, budget and schedule.
  • Participation of employees from all important departments.
  • Proper data cleaning, testing and user training.
  • Documentation and knowledge-sharing so that the project does not depend on one person.
  • External specialist support when internal skills are insufficient.

★ Exam-ready 5-mark answer

Disadvantages: shortage of ERP expertise, longer implementation time, training requirements, risk of configuration errors, limited outside perspective and dependence on key employees.

It is suitable when an organisation has capable internal staff, management support and sufficient time. A hybrid approach can be used when specialist expertise is required.

2.1 Introduction

An ERP vendor is a company that develops, owns, licenses or supplies ERP software to organisations. The vendor provides the ERP product and may also provide product updates, technical documentation, maintenance, customer support, training resources and a network of certified implementation partners.

Organisations select an ERP vendor according to their business size, industry, budget, required modules, technology environment and future plans.

Exam definition: An ERP vendor is a software company that develops and supplies an ERP package or cloud ERP service and provides product-related updates, support and other services required to use the system.

2.2 Major responsibilities of an ERP vendor

  1. Develop ERP software: Design and maintain modules for finance, HR, purchasing, inventory, production, sales and other business functions.
  2. Provide licences or subscriptions: Make the software available under suitable commercial terms, such as perpetual licences or cloud subscriptions.
  3. Product updates and upgrades: Release fixes, security updates, new features and supported product versions.
  4. Technical documentation: Provide installation guides, configuration information, user documentation and technical references.
  5. Product support: Help customers resolve product defects and technical issues under the support agreement.
  6. Integration capabilities: Provide APIs, connectors and tools that allow the ERP system to communicate with other applications.
  7. Partner ecosystem: Work with implementation partners, consultants and technology providers who help customers deploy and extend the ERP system.
  8. Product roadmap: Communicate future product direction, supported technologies and changes that may affect customers.

2.4 Types of ERP vendors / solutions

  1. Large enterprise ERP vendors: Supply broad, highly integrated ERP suites for complex and often multinational organisations.
  2. Mid-market ERP vendors: Focus on the needs of small and medium-sized organisations, often with more focused implementation scope.
  3. Industry-specific ERP vendors: Offer solutions designed for sectors such as manufacturing, retail, construction, healthcare or distribution.
  4. Cloud ERP providers: Deliver ERP capabilities through cloud infrastructure, commonly using a subscription model.
  5. Open-source ERP providers: Offer ERP software whose source code is available under an open-source licence, subject to the licence terms.

2.5 Vendor selection process

Choosing the right ERP vendor is a major decision because the selected product may influence the organisation's processes, data, cost and technology for many years.

  1. Identify requirements: List business processes, users, locations, reporting needs and required modules.
  2. Prepare a shortlist: Identify vendors whose products match the organisation's industry, size and requirements.
  3. Evaluate functionality: Compare finance, HR, purchasing, inventory, production, sales and other required features.
  4. Check technology: Review cloud or on-premise options, integration, security, scalability, usability and data-management capabilities.
  5. Evaluate total cost: Consider software subscription or licence, implementation, infrastructure, migration, training, support and future upgrades.
  6. Check vendor capability: Examine product stability, support arrangements, implementation ecosystem, references and product roadmap.
  7. Demonstration and proof of concept: Ask shortlisted vendors to demonstrate important business scenarios using realistic requirements.
  8. Final selection and contract: Select the best-fit solution, define responsibilities, service levels, deliverables, support and exit/data arrangements.

2.8 Advantages of selecting a suitable ERP vendor

  • Access to a tested and maintained ERP product.
  • Regular updates, fixes and product improvements.
  • Availability of documentation, support and training resources.
  • Integration tools and a partner ecosystem.
  • Industry knowledge and established implementation methods.
  • Better opportunity for long-term scalability and product continuity.

2.9 Risks of selecting an unsuitable ERP vendor

  • Mismatch between product features and business requirements.
  • Unexpected implementation, customisation or subscription costs.
  • Insufficient local support or lack of skilled implementation partners.
  • Difficulty integrating with existing systems.
  • Vendor lock-in or costly migration to another product.
  • Product discontinuation, weak updates or inadequate support.

2.10 Practical example

A manufacturing company needs finance, purchasing, inventory, production planning and quality-management functions. It prepares a list of requirements, shortlists suitable ERP vendors, compares their manufacturing capabilities, checks integration with existing machines and systems, reviews the total cost and asks for a demonstration. After checking references and support arrangements, it selects a vendor and signs a contract. The vendor supplies the ERP product; an implementation team then configures and deploys it with the company's employees.

Examples of ERP vendors include SAP, Oracle, Microsoft, Infor, Sage and Epicor. Before selecting a vendor, an organisation should examine functional suitability, industry experience, technology, security, scalability, total cost, support, implementation partners and future product development.

A suitable vendor provides a reliable ERP product that supports integrated business processes. An unsuitable vendor may cause high costs, poor business fit, integration problems and long-term dependency.

3.1 Introduction

An ERP consultant is a professional or specialist who advises an organisation and helps it plan, configure, customise where necessary, test and implement an Enterprise Resource Planning system. A consultant understands both business processes and ERP technology and helps connect the organisation's requirements with the capabilities of the selected ERP package.

Consultants may work independently, for a consulting firm, or as part of an ERP vendor's implementation partner network. They may support the project from software selection through go-live and post-implementation support.

3.2 Definition

Exam-ready definition: An ERP consultant is a specialist who analyses an organisation's business requirements and provides professional guidance and services for selecting, planning, configuring, integrating, testing and implementing an ERP system.

3.3 Diagram: Roles of an ERP consultant

3.4 Types of ERP consultants

  1. Functional consultant: Specialises in a business area or ERP module such as Finance, Human Resources, Sales, Purchasing, Inventory or Production. Studies business requirements, maps processes to standard ERP functions, configures the relevant module and supports functional testing and user training.
  2. Technical consultant: Works on technical aspects such as system installation, database and infrastructure, programming, reports, interfaces, extensions, data migration and troubleshooting.
  3. Techno-functional consultant: Has knowledge of both business processes and technical features. Helps translate functional needs into technical solutions and coordinates between functional and technical teams.
  4. ERP project-management consultant: Helps plan and coordinate project scope, schedule, budget, resources, communication, risks, milestones and deliverables.
  5. ERP selection consultant: Helps an organisation define requirements, compare ERP products and vendors, assess costs and prepare a selection recommendation before purchase.

3.5 Major roles and responsibilities

  1. Understand business requirements: Meet managers and employees, study existing workflows and collect the organisation's functional and technical requirements.
  2. Analyse existing processes: Identify process problems, duplication, delays and opportunities for improvement.
  3. Process mapping and fit-gap analysis: Compare the organisation's requirements with standard ERP capabilities and identify gaps that may need process changes, configuration or approved extensions.
  4. Recommend a suitable solution: Advise on ERP modules, deployment approach, configuration options and implementation priorities.
  5. Plan the implementation: Help prepare the project plan, milestones, resources, responsibilities, risk controls and communication arrangements.
  6. Configure the ERP system: Set up business rules, master data structures, workflows, roles, approvals and module parameters according to the approved design.
  7. Support data migration: Help extract, clean, map, validate and load data from old systems into the new ERP system.
  8. Coordinate integration and testing: Help connect ERP with other applications and support functional, integration and user-acceptance testing.
  9. Train users: Prepare training material, demonstrate processes and train key users and end-users.
  10. Support go-live: Assist with the transition to the live system, resolve implementation issues and monitor early operations.
  11. Provide post-implementation support: Help investigate problems, stabilise the system, transfer knowledge and recommend improvements.

3.7 Skills required by an ERP consultant

  • Business knowledge: Understanding of finance, purchasing, sales, inventory, production or HR processes.
  • ERP product knowledge: Knowledge of ERP modules, configuration and integration.
  • Analytical skills: Ability to study processes, identify gaps and solve problems.
  • Communication: Ability to discuss requirements with managers, technical teams and users.
  • Project-management skills: Planning, coordination, documentation, time and risk management.
  • Training and interpersonal skills: Ability to explain system changes and help employees adapt.
  • Technical skills: Depending on the role, knowledge of databases, data migration, reporting, interfaces, programming or infrastructure.

3.8 Advantages of using ERP consultants

  1. Provides specialist knowledge and implementation experience.
  2. Helps translate business requirements into a workable ERP design.
  3. Can reduce avoidable configuration and implementation errors.
  4. Supports data migration, testing, integration and training.
  5. Brings structured project methods, documentation and risk management.
  6. Can transfer knowledge to the organisation's internal team.

3.9 Limitations and risks

  • Consulting services can increase the total project cost.
  • The organisation may become too dependent on external specialists.
  • A consultant may not fully understand the organisation's culture or every operational detail.
  • Miscommunication between consultants and employees can lead to an unsuitable solution.
  • Excessive customisation recommended without proper analysis can increase complexity and maintenance cost.
  • Knowledge may leave with the consultant if documentation and knowledge transfer are weak.

4.1 Introduction

An ERP end-user is an employee or authorised person who uses the ERP system to perform regular business activities. End-users enter, update, retrieve and verify information through ERP modules according to their job responsibilities. Examples include accountants, purchase officers, storekeepers, sales executives, production supervisors, HR officers and managers.

End-users are important because ERP software is useful only when people use it correctly and consistently. They work with the live system and provide practical feedback about whether the configured processes meet actual business needs.

4.2 Definition

Exam-ready definition: ERP end-users are the authorised employees or business participants who interact directly with an ERP system to perform their daily tasks, record transactions, access organisational information and support integrated business operations.

4.3 Diagram: Types of ERP end-users

4.4 Types of ERP end-users

  1. Operational end-users: Perform daily transactions such as creating sales orders, recording purchases, issuing materials, entering attendance, preparing invoices and updating stock.
  2. Managerial end-users: Use reports, dashboards and summaries to monitor performance, approve transactions, allocate resources and make departmental decisions.
  3. Key users / super users: Have deeper knowledge of selected ERP modules. They help colleagues, answer routine questions, support testing and communicate issues to the ERP project team.
  4. Occasional or self-service users: Use the ERP system only when required, for example to submit leave requests, approve expenses, check payslips or view order status.
  5. System or application administrators: Manage user accounts, roles, access permissions and certain system settings. They are specialised users and should be distinguished from ordinary business end-users.

4.6 Major roles and responsibilities of ERP end-users

  1. Participate in requirement collection: Explain daily tasks, existing problems, information needs and departmental rules to the project team.
  2. Support process mapping: Describe how work is actually performed and identify unnecessary steps, duplicate entries and delays.
  3. Validate the system design: Review whether the proposed ERP screens, workflows, reports and approval processes are suitable for their work.
  4. Support data preparation: Help verify customer, supplier, employee, product, inventory and other business records before migration.
  5. Perform user acceptance testing (UAT): Execute realistic business scenarios and confirm whether the system supports required tasks correctly.
  6. Attend training: Learn the approved processes, system navigation, data-entry standards and security rules.
  7. Enter accurate and timely data: Record transactions correctly because errors can affect other connected departments.
  8. Follow standard procedures: Use authorised workflows and approval rules rather than bypassing system controls.
  9. Protect information: Keep passwords confidential, use only authorised access and handle business or personal information responsibly.
  10. Report problems and give feedback: Inform key users or support teams about errors, missing functions, confusing screens and improvement opportunities.
  11. Support adoption: Help colleagues adjust to the new system and encourage consistent use of the approved ERP processes.

4.8 Importance and benefits of involving end-users

  • Better requirement accuracy: Users understand the details of daily work and can identify practical needs.
  • Improved system usability: Their feedback can help make screens, reports and workflows easier to use.
  • Higher user acceptance: Participation makes employees feel involved in the change rather than having a system imposed on them.
  • Better testing: Users can identify real-life problems that technical teams may overlook.
  • Improved data quality: Employees who understand the source records can help validate migrated data and enter accurate transactions.
  • Faster issue identification: Regular users can recognise errors and process problems quickly.
  • Better return on ERP investment: The organisation gains value when employees adopt the system and use its integrated processes effectively.

4.9 Problems caused by poor end-user participation

  • Resistance to change and continued use of old manual methods.
  • Incorrect or incomplete transaction entry.
  • Insufficient participation in testing and training.
  • Workarounds that bypass approval and security controls.
  • Low adoption, repeated errors and poor-quality reports.
  • Delayed issue reporting and reduced confidence in the ERP system.

4.10 Best practices for successful end-user adoption

  1. Involve representatives from all affected departments from the beginning.
  2. Explain why the ERP system is being introduced and how work will change.
  3. Provide role-based training using realistic business examples.
  4. Use key users or super users as first-level support.
  5. Prepare simple user manuals, process guides and frequently asked questions.
  6. Provide a clear help-desk or issue-reporting process.
  7. Monitor adoption, error rates and user feedback after go-live.
  8. Recognise that training and support must continue after implementation.

5Topic 5 of 6ERP Implementation Life Cycle

RED PRIORITY — DIRECT RANCHI UNIVERSITY PYQ · 15 MARKS

“What do you understand by ERP implementation life cycle? With a neat diagram, discuss its different phases.”

5.1 Introduction

ERP implementation is a major organisational project in which an enterprise resource planning package is selected, planned, configured, tested and introduced into business operations. The ERP implementation life cycle is the structured sequence of stages followed to move from the initial decision to implement ERP to stable operation and continuous improvement.

It provides a roadmap for coordinating people, processes, data, software, infrastructure, time, cost and organisational change. The exact names and number of phases differ among ERP books, vendors and implementation methodologies. For the examination answer below, a widely taught classic academic sequence is explained; a modern SAP methodology is also noted separately.

5.2 Definition

Exam-ready definition: ERP implementation life cycle is a systematic sequence of activities and phases through which an organisation evaluates and selects an ERP package, plans the project, analyses and redesigns business processes, configures and tests the system, trains users, goes live and provides post-implementation support.

5.3 Diagram: ERP implementation life cycle (11 phases)

5.4 Phases of the classic ERP implementation life cycle

Phase 1 — Pre-evaluation screening

The organisation first examines whether it needs an ERP system and whether ERP is suitable for its size, industry, business problems and long-term goals. It studies the market, identifies broad requirements and prepares an initial business case.

  • Identify business problems such as disconnected databases, duplicate data, delayed reports or poor coordination.
  • Define broad objectives and expected benefits.
  • Estimate project size, budget, risks and readiness.
  • Decide whether ERP adoption is justified.

Output: Initial business case, broad requirements and approval to investigate ERP solutions.

Phase 2 — Package evaluation

Suitable ERP packages and vendors are identified and compared. The organisation checks whether the software supports its business processes and industry requirements.

  • Prepare functional and technical requirements.
  • Compare ERP products, modules, deployment options and technology.
  • Evaluate vendor reputation, industry experience, support and implementation partners.
  • Conduct product demonstrations and reference checks.
  • Compare licence or subscription costs, implementation expenses and total cost of ownership.

Output: Selected ERP package and vendor, with a documented selection decision.

Phase 3 — Project planning

After selecting the package, the organisation creates a detailed implementation plan. This phase establishes project governance and coordinates people, time, money and resources.

  • Appoint project sponsor, project manager and cross-functional team.
  • Define scope, objectives, milestones, budget and schedule.
  • Assign responsibilities to departments, vendor and consultants.
  • Prepare communication, risk, quality and change-management plans.
  • Identify infrastructure, integration and data requirements.

Output: Approved project plan, team structure, schedule, budget and governance arrangements.

Phase 4 — Gap analysis

Gap analysis compares the organisation's existing business processes and requirements with the standard functions available in the selected ERP package. It identifies the difference between what the organisation needs and what the package can provide without changes.

  • Study current business processes (AS-IS).
  • Study the standard ERP processes and proposed future processes (TO-BE).
  • Identify missing functions, process differences, reporting needs and integration gaps.
  • Decide whether to adopt standard ERP processes, change the business process or approve a necessary extension.

Output: Gap list, fit-gap analysis and approved solution requirements.

Phase 5 — Business Process Reengineering (BPR) / process redesign

Business Process Reengineering means fundamentally rethinking and redesigning business processes to achieve major improvements in performance, cost, quality and service. During ERP implementation, organisations may need to change old working methods to align with integrated ERP processes.

  • Remove unnecessary or duplicate activities.
  • Simplify workflows and approval procedures.
  • Define responsibilities and controls clearly.
  • Standardise processes across departments or branches where appropriate.
  • Document the redesigned process before system configuration.

Output: Redesigned business processes and an agreed process blueprint. BPR should be planned carefully; changing every process without justification can increase risk and resistance.

Phase 6 — Configuration and customisation

The selected ERP package is configured to support the approved business processes. Configuration means setting available options, rules and parameters. Customisation means changing or extending the standard product through additional code or extensions when a genuine requirement cannot be met by configuration or process change.

  • Set up company codes, business units, plants, warehouses and organisational structures.
  • Configure finance, HR, purchasing, inventory, production, sales and other required modules.
  • Set approval rules, user roles, workflows, taxes, numbering and reports.
  • Develop approved extensions, interfaces or reports where necessary.
  • Prepare master data structures and migration mappings.

Output: Configured ERP system and documented solution design. Excessive customisation should be avoided because it can increase cost and complicate upgrades.

Phase 7 — Implementation team training

The core implementation team, key users and super users receive detailed training on the ERP product, configuration and business processes. They become the internal experts who can help other employees.

  • Train functional and technical project-team members.
  • Train key users in their modules and responsibilities.
  • Explain configuration, process flows, security and support procedures.
  • Prepare training material and user guides for the wider workforce.

Output: Trained project team and key users ready to support testing, training and deployment.

Phase 8 — Testing

The configured ERP system is tested to confirm that it works correctly and supports complete business processes. Testing should cover individual functions as well as transactions that cross departments.

  • Unit / configuration testing: Check individual configured functions.
  • Integration testing: Verify that connected modules exchange information correctly.
  • Data migration testing: Confirm migrated records are complete, accurate and reconciled.
  • Performance and security testing: Check response, access controls and system behaviour under expected conditions.
  • User Acceptance Testing (UAT): Business users execute realistic scenarios and confirm that requirements are met.
  • Record defects, correct them and repeat tests where necessary.

Output: Approved test results, resolved critical defects and business acceptance to proceed.

Phase 9 — Going live / deployment

Go-live is the point at which the organisation starts using the new ERP system for real business transactions. The project team moves the approved solution and final data into the production environment.

  • Prepare a cutover plan and communicate the go-live date.
  • Complete final data migration and reconciliation.
  • Confirm users, roles, infrastructure, integrations and support coverage.
  • Switch from the old system or agreed legacy process to the new ERP system.
  • Monitor transactions closely and resolve urgent issues.

Output: ERP system operating in the production environment.

Phase 10 — End-user training and adoption

All employees who will use the ERP system receive role-based training. Training explains the tasks each user must perform and the procedures they must follow in the live system.

  • Train users according to their roles and ERP modules.
  • Demonstrate real transactions and common error-handling steps.
  • Provide user manuals, quick guides and help-desk support.
  • Explain security, passwords, approvals and data-quality responsibilities.
  • Collect feedback and provide additional support to users who need it.

Output: Users prepared to perform their duties using the ERP system. In practice, training often starts before go-live and continues after deployment; this separate phase highlights the importance of user adoption.

Phase 11 — Post-implementation support and maintenance

After go-live, the organisation stabilises the system, resolves remaining problems and checks whether expected benefits are being achieved. ERP implementation does not end on the first day of live operation.

  • Provide help-desk and technical support.
  • Monitor system performance, security, data quality and transaction errors.
  • Resolve defects and improve reports or workflows.
  • Maintain documentation and train new employees.
  • Review project objectives, costs, benefits and user feedback.
  • Plan upgrades, patches and continuous improvement.

Output: Stable ERP operations, support arrangements and a continuous-improvement plan.

5.6 Modern example: SAP Activate methodology

Modern ERP vendors may use a different phase structure from the classic academic sequence. For example, SAP Activate describes six phases:

  1. Discover: Understand solution options, business value and project scope.
  2. Prepare: Finalise planning, onboard the project team and prepare the environment.
  3. Explore: Conduct fit-to-standard workshops and confirm the solution design.
  4. Realize: Configure, build, test and validate the solution through iterations.
  5. Deploy: Complete cutover, final testing and transition to production.
  6. Run: Operate, support and continuously optimise the ERP solution.

Exam tip: Use the classic phases in Section 5.4 for the long-answer response, unless the question specifically asks about a vendor methodology such as SAP Activate. The number and names of phases vary by methodology.

5.8 Critical success factors of ERP implementation

  • Top management support: Leaders must provide direction, resources and timely decisions.
  • Clear objectives and scope: Project goals must be realistic and measurable.
  • Effective project management: Activities, milestones, costs, responsibilities and risks must be controlled.
  • Business process alignment: ERP configuration should support approved and efficient processes.
  • End-user participation: Employees must contribute to requirements, testing, training and adoption.
  • Good data quality: Incorrect or duplicate data should be cleaned before migration.
  • Suitable training and change management: Users should understand the system and the reasons for new processes.
  • Testing and risk control: Business scenarios, integrations and security should be tested before go-live.
  • Vendor and consultant support: The project needs suitable product expertise and implementation assistance.
  • Post-go-live support: Issues must be resolved and benefits monitored after deployment.

5.9 Common problems during the implementation life cycle

  • Unclear objectives and frequent scope changes.
  • Selection of an ERP package that does not fit business requirements.
  • Weak project planning or insufficient management support.
  • Resistance to process change and low employee participation.
  • Excessive customisation and complex integrations.
  • Poor data quality and incomplete migration.
  • Insufficient testing or inadequate user training.
  • Unplanned downtime, cutover problems or weak post-go-live support.

5.10 Practical example

Consider a manufacturing company that wants to integrate purchasing, inventory, production, sales and finance. First, it identifies problems caused by separate spreadsheets and delayed stock information. It evaluates ERP products and selects a suitable package. A project team then plans the work, compares current processes with ERP functions and redesigns purchase and production workflows. The team configures the modules, migrates item and supplier data, trains key users and tests the complete procure-to-pay and order-to-cash processes. After final checks, the company goes live. Employees receive role-based training and the support team monitors errors, performance and user feedback. This complete journey represents the ERP implementation life cycle.

6.1 Introduction

An ERP case study is a detailed study of how a real organisation selected, implemented, used or upgraded an Enterprise Resource Planning system. It explains the organisation's background, business problems, ERP solution, implementation approach, challenges, results and lessons learned.

Case studies help students understand how ERP concepts are applied in real organisations. They show that ERP implementation is not simply the installation of software; it also involves business-process changes, data integration, employee training, leadership, project management and continuous improvement.

6.2 Definition

Exam-ready definition: An ERP case study is a systematic analysis of an organisation's real experience with an ERP system, covering its business requirements, selected ERP solution, implementation process, challenges, outcomes and lessons learned.

6.3 Objectives of studying ERP case studies

  1. Understand practical implementation: Learn how ERP is introduced in real organisations.
  2. Identify business problems: Understand issues such as disconnected systems, duplicate data, manual work and poor reporting.
  3. Study ERP selection: See why an organisation selects a particular ERP vendor or product.
  4. Understand implementation challenges: Examine problems involving data migration, customisation, integration, training, cost and resistance to change.
  5. Evaluate results: Study improvements in efficiency, reporting, customer service, supply chain and decision-making.
  6. Learn best practices: Identify actions that can make future ERP projects more successful.

6.5 Case Study 1 — Nestlé: SAP ERP and the GLOBE programme

Company: Nestlé is a global food and beverage company operating across many countries and product categories.

Background and problem: Before its global standardisation programme, different Nestlé businesses and countries used different information systems, data structures and business processes. This made it difficult to obtain consistent information and coordinate operations across the group.

ERP solution: Nestlé launched the GLOBE (Global Business Excellence) programme around 2000, using SAP as the foundation for a common global business platform. The aim was to standardise key processes and data across functions such as finance, supply chain, factories, human resources, sales and marketing.

Implementation approach: The programme used a common global template based on standard business practices. Local changes were intended to be limited to legal, fiscal or specific business requirements. Business employees and leaders were involved in defining and adopting the common processes.

Challenges and learning: The historical rollout showed that a global ERP programme can face employee resistance, process-reengineering difficulties, training gaps and coordination problems. A widely discussed account of Nestlé USA's earlier SAP implementation described how insufficient involvement of affected business groups and inadequate preparation contributed to resistance and disruption. This is an important reminder that software configuration alone cannot ensure success.

Outcome and later development: Nestlé continued to evolve its SAP landscape. In its more recent cloud transformation, Nestlé and SAP reported a single point of entry to SAP applications for 275,000 employees, migration of more than 1,200 terabytes of data and 99.97% system availability following infrastructure simplification. These figures relate to the later cloud transformation and should not be confused with the original GLOBE rollout.

Lessons learned:

  • Global organisations benefit from common processes and standardised data.
  • Top-management support and business participation are essential.
  • Employees affected by process changes must be involved early.
  • Training, communication and change management must receive sufficient attention.
  • ERP should be treated as a business transformation programme, not merely an IT installation.

Source note: Historical GLOBE background is supported by Nestlé's published transformational history and academic case research. The later cloud metrics are from SAP's Nestlé customer story.

6.6 Case Study 2 — WayCool: SAP S/4HANA migration

Company: WayCool is an Indian food and agritech business working across the food supply chain.

Background and problem: WayCool needed an integrated technology platform to connect its business applications and support supply-chain operations across multiple plants and locations. It also wanted better access to timely business information and less manual reconciliation between systems.

ERP solution: WayCool adopted SAP S/4HANA as its core ERP platform, with the objectives of creating a modular but interconnected ecosystem, providing analytics and supporting cloud access.

Implementation approach: According to SAP's published account, a dedicated 40-member team worked on the deployment across 44 plants. The platform went live in 22 days and was stabilised over approximately 60–70 days. The project included more than 100 integrations, over 200 training sessions and support for 247 unique scenarios.

Reported results: SAP's case account describes integration of multiple platforms, reduced manual reconciliation, improved data transparency and analytical visibility, and a scalable foundation for business growth. The short 22-day go-live should be understood in the context of this particular migration and its dedicated project team; it is not a universal ERP implementation timeline.

Lessons learned:

  • A dedicated cross-functional project team helps coordinate a complex rollout.
  • Integration planning is important when many business applications are involved.
  • Training and post-go-live support are necessary even when deployment is rapid.
  • Real-time dashboards and integrated data can improve operational visibility.
  • Implementation speed should never replace testing, data quality and user readiness.

6.7 Case Study 3 — Raychem RPG: Oracle Fusion Cloud Applications

Company: Raychem RPG is an Indian engineering and manufacturing company operating in areas such as cable accessories, electrical equipment and infrastructure solutions.

Background and problem: The company wanted a unified platform to support growth, reduce overlapping applications and improve the availability of timely business information. Employees previously had to collect data from multiple systems and spreadsheets before preparing useful reports.

ERP solution: Raychem RPG selected Oracle Fusion Cloud Applications for ERP, supply chain management (SCM) and customer experience (CX).

Implementation approach: Oracle's published customer account states that Raychem RPG worked with Oracle Consulting and completed the implementation in a single 10-month project. The implementation partner contributed specialist knowledge that the company did not have entirely in-house.

Reported results: The company improved process visibility and data management, reduced the time required for purchase requisition-to-purchase-order processing, and improved competitive bidding response times by 25%. Automation also supported purchase orders, legal contracts and supplier qualification processes.

Lessons learned:

  • An integrated ERP and SCM platform can reduce fragmented business applications.
  • Working with an experienced implementation partner can fill internal skill gaps.
  • Automation and better data quality can improve procurement responsiveness.
  • ERP benefits should be measured through business outcomes, not only technical go-live.

6.9 Common success factors identified from ERP case studies

6.10 Common reasons for ERP implementation difficulties or failure

  • Lack of senior-management support or weak project governance.
  • Insufficient involvement of employees and business departments.
  • Poor data quality and incomplete data migration.
  • Excessive customisation and complex integrations.
  • Insufficient testing and inadequate end-user training.
  • Resistance to new processes and lack of change management.
  • Weak post-go-live support and failure to measure business benefits.
