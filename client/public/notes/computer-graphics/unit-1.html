2.1 Meaning and Need of Scan Converting Lines

BLACK Why study this? This is the foundation for understanding all line-generation algorithms. No matching standalone question was identified in the checked papers.

How much depth is needed: 1-mark definition and a short 5-mark explanation.

2.1.1 What is a line in computer graphics?

A line segment is a straight path joining two endpoints. In computer graphics, it is usually specified by two coordinates: starting point (x₁, y₁) and ending point (x₂, y₂).

2.1.2 What is scan conversion?

Scan conversion is the process of converting a geometric description of an object into a set of discrete pixels that can be displayed on a raster screen. For a line, the algorithm chooses which pixels should be turned on so that the displayed result looks as close as possible to the ideal straight line.

2.1.3 Why is scan conversion required?

  • A raster display contains a finite grid of pixels, not infinitely many points.
  • A mathematical line may pass between pixel centres.
  • The algorithm must select suitable pixels to make the line appear straight and continuous.
  • Good algorithms should be accurate, consistent in brightness and fast.

Remember: Scan conversion does not change the mathematical line. It decides how that line is represented on a discrete pixel grid.

2.2 Line Equation and Direct Method

YELLOW Why study this? It explains the mathematical foundation used by incremental line algorithms. The supplied older MCA/BCA material includes line-coordinate and line-generation questions; the direct method itself is supporting preparation.

How much depth is needed: Understand the equation and explain why incremental algorithms are preferred.

2.2.1 Slope-intercept form

A non-vertical straight line can be represented by:

y = mx + c

where m is the slope and c is the y-intercept. Given two endpoints:

m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁) = Δy / Δx

Then the equation can be used to calculate a y-coordinate for a selected x-coordinate. A vertical line has undefined slope and is handled separately.

2.2.2 Direct line-generation method

  1. Read the two endpoints.
  2. Calculate the slope m.
  3. Use the line equation to calculate intermediate coordinates.
  4. Round each calculated coordinate to a pixel position and plot it.

2.2.3 Limitation of the direct method

Repeated multiplication and division, floating-point calculations and rounding can make the method less efficient. It also needs special handling for vertical lines and steep slopes. Incremental methods such as DDA and Bresenham calculate each next point from the previous one and are more suitable for raster line generation.

Important: The direct equation is a mathematical explanation, not the same algorithm as DDA or Bresenham.

2.3 DDA Line Drawing Algorithm

YELLOW Why study this? DDA is one of the standard line-generation algorithms covered by the primary MCA line-generation question and by related supplied question material. The available papers do not establish a reliable percentage threshold, so this is not labelled red by frequency.

How much depth is needed: Prepare definition, formulas, algorithm, pseudocode, a complete numerical example, advantages and disadvantages. Practise a 5-mark answer and a long numerical answer.

2.3.1 Definition

DDA (Digital Differential Analyzer) is an incremental scan-conversion algorithm that generates a line by calculating small, equal increments in x and y and plotting the nearest pixel at each step.

2.3.2 Formulae used in DDA

Let the endpoints be (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂).

Δx = x₂ − x₁Change in x
Δy = y₂ − y₁Change in y
steps = max(|Δx|, |Δy|)Number of increments
x_increment = Δx / stepsChange in x per step
y_increment = Δy / stepsChange in y per step

Start with x = x₁ and y = y₁. At every iteration, plot (round(x), round(y)), then add the two increments.

2.3.3 Why do we use maximum of |Δx| and |Δy|?

At each step, the algorithm should move by no more than one pixel in either coordinate direction. Choosing the larger absolute difference as the number of steps ensures the larger coordinate changes by one unit per step, while the smaller coordinate changes by a fraction of a unit.

2.3.4 DDA algorithm — step by step

  1. Input the two endpoints (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂).
  2. Calculate Δx = x₂ − x₁ and Δy = y₂ − y₁.
  3. Calculate steps = max(|Δx|, |Δy|).
  4. Calculate x_increment = Δx / steps and y_increment = Δy / steps.
  5. Set x = x₁ and y = y₁.
  6. Plot the pixel at (round(x), round(y)).
  7. Increase x and y by their respective increments. Repeat through the final step.

2.3.5 Pseudocode

DDA(x1, y1, x2, y2)
    dx = x2 - x1
    dy = y2 - y1
    steps = max(abs(dx), abs(dy))

    x_inc = dx / steps
    y_inc = dy / steps

    x = x1
    y = y1

    for i = 0 to steps
        plot(round(x), round(y))
        x = x + x_inc
        y = y + y_inc

2.3.7 Advantages of DDA

  • Simple and easy to understand and implement.
  • Works for positive, negative, steep and shallow slopes when signed increments are used.
  • Uses incremental calculations rather than solving the full line equation for every pixel.

2.3.8 Disadvantages of DDA

  • Uses floating-point arithmetic.
  • Requires rounding at each step.
  • Repeated rounding can introduce small cumulative errors.
  • Generally less efficient than integer-based Bresenham for raster hardware.

Remember: In DDA, use max(|Δx|, |Δy|), not just Δx. This is essential for steep lines and lines in different directions.

2.4 Bresenham Line Drawing Algorithm

YELLOW Why study this? Bresenham is a major line-generation algorithm and is included in the supplied question material. Its exact frequency percentage is not claimed because the mixed-paper denominator has not been verified.

How much depth is needed: Prepare the definition, decision parameter, two cases, algorithm, pseudocode, numerical example and comparison with DDA.

2.4.1 Definition

Bresenham's line drawing algorithm is an incremental raster line-generation algorithm that selects the next pixel using an integer decision parameter. In its basic form for a line with slope between 0 and 1, it chooses between the East (E) and North-East (NE) candidate pixels.

2.4.2 Basic assumption and candidate pixels

For the simple derivation below, assume the line moves left to right, 0 ≤ m ≤ 1, x₂ > x₁ and y₂ ≥ y₁. Each step increases x by one. The next pixel is either E = (x+1, y) or NE = (x+1, y+1).

2.4.3 Decision parameter

Let Δx = x₂ − x₁ and Δy = y₂ − y₁. For the basic case 0 ≤ m ≤ 1, the initial decision parameter is:

p₀ = 2Δy − Δx

At each step:

ConditionChooseNew decision parameter
p < 0E: (x+1, y)p(next) = p + 2Δy
p ≥ 0NE: (x+1, y+1)p(next) = p + 2Δy − 2Δx

The parameter tells the algorithm which candidate pixel is closer to the ideal line. The tie case p = 0 is handled here by choosing NE; a consistent tie rule should be used throughout.

2.4.4 Algorithm steps — basic slope 0 to 1

  1. Input the two endpoints.
  2. Calculate Δx and Δy.
  3. Set p = 2Δy − Δx.
  4. Start at (x₁, y₁) and plot it.
  5. For each next x-coordinate, if p < 0, choose E and add 2Δy to p.
  6. Otherwise choose NE and add 2Δy − 2Δx to p.
  7. Plot each selected pixel until the endpoint is reached.

2.4.5 Pseudocode

Bresenham(x1, y1, x2, y2)
    dx = x2 - x1
    dy = y2 - y1
    p = 2 * dy - dx
    x = x1
    y = y1
    plot(x, y)

    while x < x2
        x = x + 1
        if p < 0
            p = p + 2 * dy
        else
            y = y + 1
            p = p + 2 * dy - 2 * dx
        plot(x, y)

2.4.7 Advantages

  • Uses integer arithmetic in the basic algorithm.
  • Avoids repeated floating-point multiplication and division.
  • Efficient and accurate for raster line generation.
  • Incremental decision updates are simple.

2.4.8 Disadvantages / limitations

  • The basic form above is derived for a line with slope between 0 and 1.
  • Other slopes and directions need a generalised version that handles steepness and signs.
  • The decision rule must be adapted when x is not the major direction.

2.4.9 Handling other slopes

For a complete line-drawing implementation, first determine whether |Δy| > |Δx|. If so, interchange the roles of x and y (steep line). Also determine the signs of Δx and Δy to select the direction of travel. The core idea remains the same: use an integer decision variable to choose the nearest candidate pixel.

Remember: Do not apply the simple formula p₀ = 2Δy − Δx blindly to every possible slope. State the basic-case assumption in an exam answer, or explain that the generalised algorithm handles all octants.

3Topic 3 of 7Scan Converting CirclesRED PRIORITY

RED — DIRECT MCA PYQ EVIDENCE Why this topic matters: The supplied MCA 2022–24 paper (CCMCA403) asks students to draw a circle using C++ or Java. An older MCA paper also includes circle-coordinate questions. The circle-generation algorithm is therefore a direct exam-preparation priority.

How much depth is needed: Learn the circle equation, eight-way symmetry, Bresenham’s circle algorithm, decision parameter, both update cases, a complete numerical example, pseudocode and a C++-style implementation.

3.1 What is circle scan conversion?

A mathematical circle is a continuous curve. A raster display, however, is made of discrete pixels. Circle scan conversion means selecting a set of pixels that represent the circumference as closely as possible.

A circle with centre (xc, yc) and radius r is described by:

(x − xc)² + (y − yc)² = r²

For a circle centred at the origin, this becomes x² + y² = r².

3.2 Why do we calculate only one-eighth of the circle?

A circle has eight-way symmetry. If one point (x, y) is on a circle centred at the origin, then the following seven reflected or swapped-coordinate points are also on the same circle:

(x,y), (y,x), (−x,y), (−y,x), (−x,−y), (−y,−x), (x,−y), (y,−x)

These eight positions lie in eight 45-degree regions called octants. So the algorithm calculates pixels only in the first octant, from (0,r) towards the diagonal x = y. Each calculated point is reflected to obtain the other seven positions.

Why this saves work: Without symmetry, we would calculate points around the entire circumference. With eight-way symmetry, we calculate approximately one-eighth and reflect each point. The axis and diagonal points overlap, so duplicate plotted pixels may occur and can be ignored.

3.3 Bresenham’s Circle Drawing Algorithm

Definition: Bresenham’s circle drawing algorithm is an incremental raster algorithm that selects the next pixel on a circle by using an integer decision parameter. It uses additions and subtractions rather than repeatedly evaluating the circle equation with floating-point calculations.

For the version used in these notes, the circle is initially assumed to be centred at the origin. We begin at the top point (0,r) and move through the first octant.

3.4 Candidate pixels and decision parameter

At each iteration, x increases by 1. The next pixel is chosen from two candidates:

  • East (E): (x + 1, y) — x increases, y stays the same.
  • South-East (SE): (x + 1, y − 1) — x increases and y decreases.

The decision parameter tells us which candidate is closer to the true circular boundary. For the integer form used here, the initial value is:

p₀ = 3 − 2r

3.5 Decision rules and update formulas

Start with x = 0, y = r and p = 3 − 2r. Then apply the following rule. In the formulas below, x′ and y′ mean the new coordinates after the move.

3.6 Complete algorithm — step by step

  1. Read the radius r and centre (x₀,y₀).
  2. Set x = 0 and y = r.
  3. Set the initial decision parameter p = 3 − 2r.
  4. Plot the eight symmetric points around the required centre.
  5. While x ≤ y, check the decision parameter.
  6. If p < 0, choose E: increase x by 1 and keep y unchanged; update p = p + 4x + 6 using the new x.
  7. Otherwise choose SE: increase x by 1, decrease y by 1; update p = p + 4(x − y) + 10 using the new x and new y.
  8. Plot the eight symmetric points for the new coordinate pair.
  9. Stop when x > y. The first-octant calculation is complete.

Loop-condition note: The point where x = y belongs to the calculated octant and should be plotted once. Therefore, a convenient implementation uses while (x ≤ y) and checks the condition again after updating the coordinates.

3.12 Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Uses integer decision calculations in this form.
  • Uses additions and subtractions instead of repeated multiplication, division and square roots.
  • Uses eight-way symmetry, so only one octant needs to be calculated.
  • Incremental updates make it efficient for raster displays.
  • Produces a consistent sequence of pixels around the circle.

Limitations

  • The basic derivation assumes an integer radius and a circle aligned to the pixel coordinate system.
  • It generates a raster approximation, not a mathematically continuous curve.
  • Duplicate pixels can occur on axes and on the 45-degree diagonal; these should be treated as one visible pixel.
  • The plotting function depends on the graphics library or display system being used.

4.1 What is an ellipse?

An ellipse is a closed, oval-shaped curve. It can be understood as a stretched or compressed circle. It has a centre, a horizontal radius and a vertical radius. These two radii are usually written as rx and ry.

  • Horizontal radius (rx): Distance from the centre to the ellipse boundary along the x-axis.
  • Vertical radius (ry): Distance from the centre to the ellipse boundary along the y-axis.
  • Centre (xc, yc): The point around which the ellipse is symmetric.
  • Major axis: The longer diameter of the ellipse.
  • Minor axis: The shorter diameter of the ellipse.

4.2 Mathematical equation of an ellipse

For an ellipse centred at the origin with its axes parallel to the coordinate axes:

x²/rx² + y²/ry² = 1

For an ellipse centred at (xc, yc):

(x − xc)²/rx² + (y − yc)²/ry² = 1

To make the decision calculations easier, multiply the origin-centred equation by rx²ry² and define the implicit function:

F(x,y) = ry²x² + rx²y² − rx²ry²

How to read F(x,y): If F(x,y) = 0, the point lies on the ideal ellipse. If F(x,y) < 0, it is inside. If F(x,y) > 0, it is outside. The midpoint algorithm evaluates this function at carefully selected midpoints to decide which pixel is closer to the curve.

4.3 Why do we calculate only one quadrant?

An axis-aligned ellipse is symmetric about both the x-axis and the y-axis. Therefore, every point in the first quadrant has three matching points in the other quadrants. We calculate one quadrant and reflect each point to obtain the full ellipse. This is called four-way symmetry.

For an ellipse centred at the origin, the four points are:

(x,y), (−x,y), (x,−y), (−x,−y)

For a centre at (xc, yc), add the centre coordinates:

(xc+x, yc+y), (xc−x, yc+y), (xc+x, yc−y), (xc−x, yc−y)

4.4 Why are there two regions?

The slope of the ellipse changes as we move around the first quadrant. Near the top of the ellipse, the curve is relatively flat: x changes at each step while y may stay the same or decrease. Near the right-hand side, the curve becomes steep: y decreases at each step while x may stay the same or increase.

To handle these different behaviours efficiently, the first quadrant is divided into two regions at the point where the tangent slope is −1.

Differentiate the ellipse equation:

x²/rx² + y²/ry² = 1
2x/rx² + (2y/ry²)(dy/dx) = 0
dy/dx = −(ry²x)/(rx²y)

At the region boundary, the slope is −1. Therefore:

ry²x = rx²y

Equivalently, compare the doubled terms:

2ry²x = 2rx²y
RegionDirection of progressCandidate pixelsDecision idea
Region 1x increases by 1E: (x+1,y) or SE: (x+1,y−1)Choose whether y stays or decreases.
Region 2y decreases by 1S: (x,y−1) or SE: (x+1,y−1)Choose whether x stays or increases.

Region 1 condition: continue while 2ry²x < 2rx²y. Switch to Region 2 when this condition becomes false.

4.5 Midpoint Ellipse Drawing Algorithm — the central idea

The Midpoint Ellipse Drawing Algorithm is an incremental raster algorithm. Instead of calculating every point from the ellipse equation, it tests the midpoint between two candidate pixels. The sign of the decision parameter tells us which candidate is closer to the ideal ellipse boundary.

Start at the top of the first quadrant: (x,y) = (0,ry). Then process Region 1 and Region 2. For each calculated point, plot its four symmetric points.

Important difference from circle drawing: A circle uses eight-way symmetry. An axis-aligned ellipse uses four-way symmetry. Also, the ellipse algorithm needs two regions because its slope changes across the quadrant.

4.6 Region 1 — initial decision parameter and updates

Initial point:

x = 0,   y = ry

The initial midpoint decision parameter for Region 1 is:

p1,0 = ry² − rx²ry + ¼rx²

At every step, x increases by one. There are two cases:

ConditionChoose pixelCoordinate updateDecision update
p₁ < 0East (E)x = x+1; y stays the samep₁ = p₁ + 2rᵧ²x + rᵧ²
p₁ ≥ 0South-East (SE)x = x+1; y = y−1p₁ = p₁ + 2rᵧ²x − 2rₓ²y + rᵧ²

In the update formulas, use the new x and y values after choosing the next pixel. The algorithm continues until the Region 1 condition 2ry²x < 2rx²y is no longer true.

Incremental terms: To avoid recalculating squares repeatedly, maintain:

  • dx = 2rᵧ²x; whenever x increases, add 2rᵧ².
  • dy = 2rₓ²y; whenever y decreases, subtract 2rₓ².

4.7 Region 2 — initial decision parameter and updates

Region 2 begins at the last point calculated in Region 1, say (x₀,y₀). Its initial decision parameter is:

p2,0 = ry²(x₀+½)² + rx²(y₀−1)² − rx²ry²

At every Region 2 step, y decreases by one. There are two cases:

ConditionChoose pixelCoordinate updateDecision update
p₂ > 0South (S)y decreases by 1; x stays the samep₂ = p₂ − 2rₓ²y + rₓ²
p₂ ≤ 0South-East (SE)x increases by 1; y decreases by 1p₂ = p₂ + 2rᵧ²x − 2rₓ²y + rₓ²

Again, use the new coordinate values in the update. Continue until y = 0. The four-way symmetry points are plotted at every step in both regions.

Remember the sign rules: Region 1: negative chooses E, otherwise SE. Region 2: positive chooses S, otherwise SE. The inequality directions are different—do not mix them up.

4.9 Algorithm steps — exam presentation

  1. Read the centre (xc,yc) and radii rx, ry.
  2. Calculate rx², ry², and initialise x=0, y=ry.
  3. Calculate p₁ = ry² − rx²ry + ¼rx².
  4. In Region 1, while 2ry²x < 2rx²y, choose E if p₁<0; otherwise choose SE. Update the decision parameter.
  5. At each point, plot the four symmetric pixels around the centre.
  6. At the last Region 1 point, calculate p₂ using the Region 2 initial formula.
  7. In Region 2, while y≥0, choose S if p₂>0; otherwise choose SE. Update the decision parameter.
  8. Plot four symmetric pixels for each Region 2 point. Stop when y=0.

#include <iostream>

void plotFourPoints(int xc, int yc, int x, int y) {

    cout << "(" << xc - x << "," << yc + y << ") ";

void midpointEllipse(int xc, int yc, int rx, int ry) {
    double rx2 = rx * rx;
    double ry2 = ry * ry;

    int x = 0;
    int y = ry;
    double dx = 0;
    double dy = 2 * rx2 * y;

    double p1 = ry2 - rx2 * ry + 0.25 * rx2;

    // Region 1
    while (dx < dy) {
        plotFourPoints(xc, yc, x, y);

        x++;
        dx += 2 * ry2;

        if (p1 < 0) {
            p1 += dx + ry2;
        } else {
            y--;
            dy -= 2 * rx2;
            p1 += dx - dy + ry2;
        }
    }

    // Region 2
    double p2 = ry2 * (x + 0.5) * (x + 0.5)
              + rx2 * (y - 1) * (y - 1)
              - rx2 * ry2;

    while (y >= 0) {
        plotFourPoints(xc, yc, x, y);

        y--;
        dy -= 2 * rx2;

        if (p2 > 0) {
            p2 += rx2 - dy;
        } else {
            x++;
            dx += 2 * ry2;
            p2 += dx - dy + rx2;
        }
    }
}

4.12 Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Uses incremental calculations instead of solving the ellipse equation independently for every pixel.
  • Uses symmetry to calculate one quadrant and reflect points into the other three.
  • Decision parameters select pixels near the ideal ellipse boundary.
  • Efficient for raster displays because repeated calculations are reduced.

Limitations

  • The standard form assumes an axis-aligned ellipse; a rotated ellipse needs additional transformation or another method.
  • Two regions and two decision parameters make it more involved than the basic circle algorithm.
  • Care is needed when switching regions and when using the correct inequality in each region.

4.17 Quick revision — remember these formulas

Ellipse equation: x²/rₓ² + y²/rᵧ² = 1

Implicit function: F(x,y)=rᵧ²x²+rₓ²y²−rₓ²rᵧ²

Four symmetric points: (±x,±y), with all four sign combinations.

Region boundary: 2rᵧ²x = 2rₓ²y

Region 1 initial: p₁,₀=rᵧ²−rₓ²rᵧ+¼rₓ²

Region 1 decisions: p₁<0 → E; p₁≥0 → SE.

Region 2 initial: p₂,₀=rᵧ²(x₀+½)²+rₓ²(y₀−1)²−rₓ²rᵧ²

Region 2 decisions: p₂>0 → S; p₂≤0 → SE.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using eight-way symmetry for an ellipse. The standard axis-aligned ellipse uses four-way symmetry.
  • Forgetting to divide the first quadrant into two regions.
  • Switching regions using the wrong comparison. Use 2rᵧ²x < 2rₓ²y for Region 1.
  • Mixing the sign rules: Region 1 uses negative → E; Region 2 uses positive → S.
  • Using the original x and y values instead of the new values in the parameter update formulas.
  • Forgetting to add the centre coordinates when the ellipse is not centred at the origin.
  • Calling the older polynomial-method PYQ a direct midpoint-ellipse PYQ. The paper's method wording must be preserved.

5.1 What Does Area Filling Mean?

In raster graphics, drawing the boundary of a shape and filling its inside are two different operations. A line algorithm decides which pixels represent the outline. An area-filling algorithm decides which pixels belong to the interior and assigns them a colour or a pattern.

Imagine drawing a closed rectangle in a paint program. The outline may be black, while the inside is blue. The algorithm must identify all interior pixels without colouring pixels outside the rectangle. The same problem occurs with polygons, ellipses, icons, maps, charts and game graphics.

Figure 5.1 — Boundary drawing and area filling are separate raster operations.

Two main approaches to area filling

ApproachHow it worksTypical algorithms
Scan-line approachMove a horizontal scan line across the shape, calculate boundary intersections and fill the spans between intersection pairs.Scan-line polygon fill; scan-line filling of rectangles and ellipses.
Seed-fill approachStart from a known interior pixel (seed) and expand to neighbouring pixels while a colour or boundary condition permits.Boundary-fill and flood-fill.

Easy memory trick: Scan-line filling asks, “Which horizontal spans are inside?” Seed filling asks, “Starting from this inside pixel, which neighbouring pixels can I reach?”

5.2 Filling Rectangles

A rectangle is one of the simplest filled areas because its horizontal limits are constant on every scan line. Suppose its opposite corners are \((x_{\min},y_{\min})\) and \((x_{\max},y_{\max})\). Every pixel whose coordinates lie inside these limits belongs to the rectangle.

For each y = y_min ... y_max:
  For each x = x_min ... x_max:
    SetPixel(x, y, fillColour)

In an actual raster implementation, the right and top edges may be treated as exclusive to avoid drawing shared edges twice when adjacent rectangles are filled. The exact convention depends on the graphics API and coordinate system.

Figure 5.2 — A rectangle can be filled by drawing a horizontal span on each row.

Algorithm: Rectangle Fill

  1. Read the two opposite corners of the rectangle.
  2. Ensure the minimum and maximum x and y values are correctly ordered.
  3. Start at the first scan line, \(y=y_{\min}\).
  4. For every row, visit each x-coordinate from \(x_{\min}\) to \(x_{\max}\).
  5. Write the fill colour to each pixel.
  6. Move to the next row and repeat until the last row is processed.
rectangleFill(x1, y1, x2, y2, colour):
    xmin = min(x1, x2)
    xmax = max(x1, x2)
    ymin = min(y1, y2)
    ymax = max(y1, y2)

    for y = ymin to ymax:
        for x = xmin to xmax:
            setPixel(x, y, colour)

Time complexity: \(O(W \times H)\), where \(W=x_{\max}-x_{\min}+1\) and \(H=y_{\max}-y_{\min}+1\). Every pixel in the rectangle is written once. Space complexity: \(O(1)\) extra space for the simple nested-loop method.

5.3 Polygon Filling

A polygon is a closed 2D shape made from straight line segments called edges. A triangle, quadrilateral, pentagon and irregular closed shape are polygons. A polygon fill algorithm colours the pixels that lie inside its boundary.

Filling becomes more interesting when a polygon is concave (it has an inward dent). A horizontal scan line may enter and leave the polygon several times. Therefore, the algorithm must find every intersection, sort them from left to right and fill the correct pairs.

Figure 5.3 — A concave polygon can produce more than one inside span on a scan line.

5.3.1 Odd–Even Rule (Even–Odd Rule)

The odd–even rule is a way to decide whether a point is inside a polygon. Imagine drawing a ray from the point toward infinity. Count how many times the ray crosses the polygon boundary:

  • Odd number of crossings: the point is inside.
  • Even number of crossings: the point is outside.

For scan-line filling, sort the intersections from left to right. The first crossing changes the state from outside to inside; the second changes it back to outside. So fill between intersection 1 and 2, 3 and 4, and so on.

Sorted intersections: x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄, ...
Fill: [x₁, x₂], [x₃, x₄], ...

This is also called the parity rule because each boundary crossing toggles the inside/outside state.

5.3.2 Scan-Line Polygon Fill Algorithm

This is the main geometric polygon-filling method. A horizontal line called a scan line moves from the polygon’s lowest y-coordinate to its highest y-coordinate. At each row, the algorithm finds where that row intersects polygon edges and fills the pixels between successive pairs.

Step-by-step working

  1. Find the minimum and maximum y-coordinate of the polygon.
  2. For the current scan line, find intersections with all relevant non-horizontal polygon edges.
  3. Sort the intersection x-coordinates in increasing order.
  4. Pair the intersections: first with second, third with fourth, and so on.
  5. Fill pixels between each pair using the chosen pixel-rounding convention.
  6. Move to the next scan line and repeat.

Figure 5.4 — A scan line intersects polygon edges; the interior interval is filled.

Worked example — pairing intersections

Suppose one scan line crosses a polygon at x-coordinates 2, 5, 9 and 14. After sorting, there are four intersections. Apply the odd–even rule:

PairSpanAction
1st and 2nd2 to 5Fill the first inside interval.
3rd and 4th9 to 14Fill the second inside interval.

Coordinates strictly between the endpoints are unambiguous. In an actual raster program, use a consistent rule such as a left-inclusive/right-exclusive span to prevent adjacent spans from drawing a shared boundary pixel twice.

5.3.3 Edge Table (ET) and Active Edge Table (AET)

Checking every polygon edge again for every scan line is wasteful. An efficient implementation uses two data structures:

  • Edge Table (ET): stores each non-horizontal edge in a bucket indexed by the edge’s lower y-coordinate, \(y_{\min}\).
  • Active Edge Table (AET): stores edges that intersect the current scan line. The AET is sorted by their current x-intersection.

An edge record commonly stores:

FieldMeaning
\(y_{\max}\)Upper endpoint y-coordinate; the edge is no longer active at its upper limit under the common half-open convention.
\(x\)Current x-intersection with the scan line.
\(1/m\)Inverse slope, used to update x when the scan line moves up one row.
m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)
1/m = (x₂ − x₁) / (y₂ − y₁)
x at next scan line = x current + 1/m

For a vertical edge, \(x\) does not change as y changes, so its inverse-slope increment is zero.

ET/AET procedure

  1. Ignore horizontal edges for intersection counting; they do not cross a scan line in the usual parity calculation.
  2. For each remaining edge, identify its lower endpoint and upper endpoint.
  3. Insert its record into the ET bucket for \(y_{\min}\).
  4. Start at the smallest non-empty ET scan-line bucket with an empty AET.
  5. At each scan line, add edges that begin there to the AET.
  6. Remove edges whose upper endpoint has been reached, following one consistent half-open endpoint convention.
  7. Sort the AET by current x-intersection.
  8. Fill between intersection pairs.
  9. Increase y by one and update each active edge’s x by adding \(1/m\).
  10. Continue until both ET and AET are empty.
scanLinePolygonFill(polygon):
    ET = buildEdgeTable(polygon)
    AET = empty
    y = smallestScanline(ET)

    while ET is not empty or AET is not empty:
        add ET[y] edges to AET
        remove edges that end at this scanline
        sort AET by current_x

        for i = 0 to length(AET)-1 step 2:
            fillSpan(AET[i].x, AET[i+1].x, y)

        for each edge in AET:
            edge.x = edge.x + edge.inverseSlope

        y = y + 1

Important implementation note: endpoint handling must be consistent. A common rule treats an edge as active for \(y_{\min} \le y < y_{\max}\), so a shared polygon vertex is not counted twice accidentally. Horizontal edges are skipped in the crossing list. When converting real-valued intersections to pixels, choose a documented rounding/span convention and apply it consistently.

Figure 5.5 — The ET supplies edges; the AET tracks the edges crossing the current row.

5.4 Boundary-Fill Algorithm

Boundary-fill is a seed-fill algorithm. It starts from a selected pixel inside a closed region and spreads outward. The algorithm colours neighbouring pixels until it reaches pixels having the specified boundary colour.

It assumes that the region is enclosed by a clearly defined boundary colour. For example, if a shape has a black outline and a white interior, choose an interior seed pixel and fill the white area with blue. The black boundary stops the process.

Figure 5.6 — Boundary-fill begins at an interior seed and stops at the boundary colour.

4-connected and 8-connected boundary-fill

Connectivity defines which neighbouring pixels the algorithm checks.

Figure 5.7 — 4-connected checks only the four sides; 8-connected also checks the four diagonals.

ConnectivityNeighbour pixelsCoordinates relative to (x,y)
4-connectedTop, bottom, left, right(x+1,y), (x−1,y), (x,y+1), (x,y−1)
8-connectedAll four sides plus four diagonalsFour side neighbours and (x±1,y±1)

Boundary-fill pseudocode — 4-connected

boundaryFill4(x, y, fillColour, boundaryColour):
    current = getPixel(x, y)

    if current != boundaryColour AND current != fillColour:
        setPixel(x, y, fillColour)

        boundaryFill4(x + 1, y, fillColour, boundaryColour)
        boundaryFill4(x - 1, y, fillColour, boundaryColour)
        boundaryFill4(x, y + 1, fillColour, boundaryColour)
        boundaryFill4(x, y - 1, fillColour, boundaryColour)

The 8-connected version makes the same test but also calls the function for the four diagonal neighbours. The fill-colour check prevents the algorithm from repeatedly processing pixels that have already been filled.

Limitations and precautions

  • The boundary must be closed. A tiny gap can allow the fill to leak outside.
  • The boundary colour must be distinguishable from the fill colour.
  • Recursive implementations can exceed the call-stack limit for large regions. An explicit stack or queue is safer in production software.
  • 4-connected fill can leave diagonal gaps in some pixel arrangements. 8-connected fill reaches diagonal neighbours, but can leak through diagonal boundary gaps. Connectivity must be chosen consistently with the boundary representation.

5.5 Flood-Fill Algorithm

Flood-fill also begins from a seed pixel, but its stopping condition is different. Instead of stopping when it encounters a specified boundary colour, it replaces a connected region of a specified old/interior colour with a new fill colour.

Think of the paint-bucket tool in an image editor. If you click on a white region, the tool changes the connected white pixels to blue. It does not need to know the colour of the outline; it checks whether each pixel still has the target old colour.

Figure 5.8 — Flood-fill replaces all connected pixels matching the old colour.

Flood-fill pseudocode — 4-connected

floodFill4(x, y, oldColour, newColour):
    current = getPixel(x, y)

    if current != oldColour:
        return

    setPixel(x, y, newColour)

    floodFill4(x + 1, y, oldColour, newColour)
    floodFill4(x - 1, y, oldColour, newColour)
    floodFill4(x, y + 1, oldColour, newColour)
    floodFill4(x, y - 1, oldColour, newColour)

Special case: If oldColour equals newColour, return immediately. Otherwise, the algorithm may revisit pixels indefinitely or perform unnecessary work, depending on the implementation.

Iterative flood-fill using a stack

For a large image, recursive flood-fill may create thousands or millions of nested function calls. An iterative version stores pending pixels in an explicit stack. This avoids relying on the programming language's call stack.

floodFillIterative(seedX, seedY, oldColour, newColour):
    if oldColour == newColour:
        return

    stack = empty stack
    push(stack, (seedX, seedY))

    while stack is not empty:
        (x, y) = pop(stack)

        if pixel(x, y) != oldColour:
            continue

        setPixel(x, y, newColour)

        push(stack, (x + 1, y))
        push(stack, (x - 1, y))
        push(stack, (x, y + 1))
        push(stack, (x, y - 1))

In real code, check image boundaries before reading or pushing neighbours. A visited array can be used when pixels are not modified immediately or when the algorithm needs additional state.

5.6 Filling an Ellipse

An ellipse can be filled by processing one horizontal scan line at a time. For an axis-aligned ellipse centred at \((x_c,y_c)\), with horizontal radius \(r_x\) and vertical radius \(r_y\), its equation is:

(x − x_c)² / rₓ² + (y − y_c)² / rᵧ² = 1

For a selected scan line y, rearrange the equation to calculate the horizontal distance from the centre:

x = x_c ± rₓ √[1 − (y − y_c)² / rᵧ²]

The two values give the left and right intersections of the scan line with the ellipse. Fill the pixels between them. Only scan lines satisfying \(|y-y_c| \le r_y\) intersect the ellipse.

Figure 5.9 — Ellipse filling calculates a left and right intersection for each valid row.

Algorithm: Scan-line ellipse fill

  1. Read the ellipse centre \((x_c,y_c)\) and radii \(r_x,r_y\).
  2. Start at \(y=y_c-r_y\) and continue through \(y_c+r_y\).
  3. For the current y, calculate \(q=1-(y-y_c)^2/r_y^2\).
  4. Calculate \(d=r_x\sqrt{q}\).
  5. Find the left and right intersections: \(x_L=x_c-d\), \(x_R=x_c+d\).
  6. Fill the integer pixels between the rounded left and right limits.
  7. Repeat for the next scan line.
fillEllipse(xc, yc, rx, ry, colour):
    for y = yc - ry to yc + ry:
        q = 1 - ((y - yc) * (y - yc)) / (ry * ry)

        if q >= 0:
            dx = rx * sqrt(q)
            left  = ceil(xc - dx)
            right = floor(xc + dx)

            for x = left to right:
                setPixel(x, y, colour)

Note: This direct equation-based method is easy to understand, but it uses square roots and floating-point arithmetic. Faster raster implementations can reuse symmetry or incremental ellipse calculations from the midpoint ellipse method, then fill horizontal spans between symmetric boundary points.

Approximate time complexity: \(O(r_y+r_xr_y)\) for the simple loop, because it processes about \(2r_y+1\) rows and writes the interior pixels. The number of writes is proportional to the ellipse's raster area.

5.7 Pattern Filling

Pattern filling fills an area with a repeating design instead of one solid colour. A pattern may be a small bitmap tile containing stripes, dots, checks, bricks, texture or another repeated arrangement.

The pattern is repeated across the interior of the shape. The polygon or other shape still determines which pixels are inside; the pattern determines the colour/value written to those inside pixels.

Figure 5.10 — A repeating checker pattern is visible only inside the polygon.

How pattern coordinates are calculated

Assume the pattern tile has width \(P_w\) and height \(P_h\). For a screen pixel \((x,y)\), calculate the corresponding tile coordinate:

patternX = (x − originX) mod P_w
patternY = (y − originY) mod P_h

Read the pattern pixel at \((patternX,patternY)\), then write that value to the screen pixel if \((x,y)\) is inside the filled shape. The modulo operation makes the pattern repeat. In languages where a negative remainder can be negative, use a positive-modulo function so the pattern index remains in the valid range.

Pattern-fill procedure

  1. Define or load a small pattern tile.
  2. Determine the interior pixels using a polygon scan-line method or another area-fill method.
  3. For every interior pixel, map its screen coordinate to a tile coordinate using modulo.
  4. Read the corresponding pattern colour.
  5. Write that colour to the screen pixel. If the pattern supports transparency, leave transparent tile pixels unchanged.
patternFill(interiorPixels, pattern, patternWidth, patternHeight):
    for each pixel (x, y) in interiorPixels:
        px = positiveModulo(x - originX, patternWidth)
        py = positiveModulo(y - originY, patternHeight)

        colour = pattern[py][px]

        if colour is not transparent:
            setPixel(x, y, colour)

Applications: texture and material representation, map symbols, backgrounds, hatching in technical drawings, surface decoration and fills in paint or drawing software.

5.9 Advantages and Disadvantages

Scan-line polygon filling

  • Advantages: efficient for polygons; handles convex and concave shapes; ET/AET avoids testing every edge from scratch on every row.
  • Disadvantages: vertex and horizontal-edge handling is subtle; self-intersecting polygons need a clearly selected fill rule; rounding can cause small raster gaps if inconsistent.

Seed-fill methods

  • Advantages: conceptually simple; useful when a seed point is available; works naturally for irregular connected regions.
  • Disadvantages: may use significant memory; recursive versions can overflow; boundary-fill depends on a closed boundary and correct colour tests.

Pattern filling

  • Advantages: produces textures and visual detail; reusable small tiles reduce the need to store a large image for a repeated design.
  • Disadvantages: pattern alignment can look inconsistent if the origin changes; transparency and tile boundaries need careful handling.

5.10 Exam-Ready Answers

2-Mark Answer — What is area filling?

Area filling is the process of assigning a colour or pattern to all raster pixels lying inside a closed graphical object. Common methods include scan-line filling, boundary-fill, flood-fill and pattern filling.

5-Mark Answer — Explain scan-line polygon filling.

Scan-line polygon filling fills a polygon row by row. A horizontal scan line is moved through the polygon. For each row, its intersections with polygon edges are calculated and sorted by increasing x-coordinate. The pixels between the first and second intersections, third and fourth intersections, and so on are filled according to the odd–even rule. For efficiency, an Edge Table stores edges by their minimum y-coordinate and an Active Edge Table stores edges intersecting the current scan line. The x-intersection is updated for the next row using the inverse slope \(1/m\). Horizontal edges and shared vertices must be handled consistently to avoid counting a vertex twice.

5-Mark Answer — Differentiate between boundary-fill and flood-fill.

Boundary-fill begins from an interior seed pixel and spreads until it encounters a specified boundary colour. Flood-fill begins from a seed pixel and replaces all connected pixels having a specified old colour. Boundary-fill therefore checks the boundary colour, whereas flood-fill checks the interior colour. Both can use 4-connected or 8-connected neighbourhoods. Boundary-fill is suitable for a closed outline of known colour, while flood-fill is commonly used by paint-bucket tools to replace a connected colour region.

5-Mark Answer — Explain 4-connected and 8-connected filling.

In 4-connected filling, each pixel checks its four direct neighbours: top, bottom, left and right. In 8-connected filling, it checks these four neighbours plus the four diagonal neighbours. Thus, 8-connected filling can reach diagonal pixels that 4-connected filling cannot. However, connectivity must be selected carefully because diagonal gaps in a boundary may allow a fill to leak.

15-Mark Answer — Explain polygon area-filling methods.

Polygon area filling assigns colour or a pattern to the interior pixels of a polygon. The main approaches are scan-line filling and seed filling.

Scan-line polygon fill: A horizontal scan line intersects polygon edges. The intersection x-coordinates are sorted, and pixels between successive pairs are filled using the odd–even rule. The Edge Table (ET) stores edges by their lower y-coordinate, while the Active Edge Table (AET) maintains edges intersecting the current scan line. Each edge stores its upper y-coordinate, current x-intersection and inverse slope. As the scan line moves, edges are inserted or removed and x is incremented by \(1/m\).

Boundary-fill: It starts from an interior seed pixel and fills neighbouring pixels until the specified boundary colour is encountered. It can be 4-connected or 8-connected.

Flood-fill: It starts from a seed and replaces connected pixels matching a specified old colour with a new colour. It is used in paint-bucket operations.

Pattern filling: Instead of a solid colour, a repeating bitmap or tile is mapped over the interior. Modulo arithmetic maps each screen pixel to a coordinate in the repeating tile.

Scan-line filling is well suited to polygon geometry, while seed-fill methods are useful when an interior seed and a pixel-colour condition are available. All methods require consistent pixel and boundary handling.

5.11 Quick Revision

  • Area filling: colour or pattern the interior pixels of a closed shape.
  • Rectangle: nested loops over x and y.
  • Scan-line polygon fill: find intersections, sort them, fill alternate pairs.
  • ET: Edge Table, bucketed by \(y_{\min}\).
  • AET: Active Edge Table, edges crossing the current scan line.
  • Inverse slope: \(1/m=\Delta x/\Delta y\), used to update x for the next row.
  • Boundary-fill: stops at boundary colour.
  • Flood-fill: replaces connected old-colour pixels.
  • 4-connected: four side neighbours.
  • 8-connected: four side plus four diagonal neighbours.
  • Ellipse fill: calculate left/right x-intersections for each y-row.
  • Pattern fill: map screen coordinates to repeating tile coordinates using modulo.

5.12 Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Do not confuse drawing a polygon boundary with filling its interior.
  • Do not fill every alternate pixel. Scan-line filling fills intervals between paired intersections.
  • Do not forget to sort intersections from left to right before pairing them.
  • Do not count horizontal edges as ordinary scan-line crossings.
  • Do not count a shared vertex twice accidentally. Use a consistent endpoint convention.
  • Do not reverse the stopping conditions: boundary-fill checks boundary colour; flood-fill checks old colour.
  • Do not assume 8-connected is always safer. It can leak through diagonal boundary gaps.
  • Do not use recursive seed-fill blindly for a huge image; prefer an explicit stack/queue or span-based implementation.
  • Do not use a negative modulo result directly as an array index in languages where remainder can be negative.

First understand what clipping means. Then learn point clipping, line clipping, polygon clipping, and finally how the same idea applies to circles and ellipses. The three algorithm names to keep separate are Cohen–Sutherland (line), Liang–Barsky (line), and Sutherland–Hodgman (polygon).

6.1 Meaning of clipping — start with a simple example

Clipping is the process of removing the part of a drawing that lies outside a selected region and keeping the part that lies inside it. The selected region is called the clipping window.

Imagine that a large picture is placed behind a small rectangular window. You can see only the portion of the picture visible through the window. Computer graphics clipping works in the same way.

Why is clipping needed?

  • To show only the required part of a scene.
  • To prevent objects outside the viewing area from appearing.
  • To remove invisible portions of lines and shapes.
  • To make window-based viewing and drawing more efficient.

6.2 Clipping window and its coordinates

For a rectangular clipping window, we specify four boundaries:

Left: xmin    Right: xmax

Bottom: ymin    Top: ymax

A point (x, y) is inside or on the window when both conditions are true:

xmin ≤ x ≤ xmax and ymin ≤ y ≤ ymax.

6.3 Point clipping — the simplest case

Point clipping checks one point at a time. If the point satisfies both coordinate conditions, it is accepted. Otherwise, it is rejected.

Accept (x, y) when:

xmin ≤ x ≤ xmax AND ymin ≤ y ≤ ymax

6.4 Line clipping — what are the possible results?

A line segment has two endpoints. When we compare the line with a rectangular window, there are three basic possibilities:

  • Completely inside: keep the complete line.
  • Completely outside: reject the complete line if it cannot cross the window.
  • Partially inside: calculate the boundary intersections and keep only the segment inside.

6.5 Cohen–Sutherland line clipping algorithm

Cohen–Sutherland is a line-clipping algorithm used with a rectangular window. Its main idea is to give each line endpoint a 4-bit region code (outcode). This code tells us where the endpoint is located in relation to the window.

Step 1: Divide the area into nine regions

The clipping window is the centre region. Around it are eight outside regions: top, bottom, left, right and four corners.

Bit order used in this diagram: TOP, BOTTOM, RIGHT, LEFT. A bit becomes 1 when the point is outside in that direction. The centre region has code 0000.

RegionOutcodeCondition
Inside0000Point is inside all four boundaries.
Top1000y > ymax
Bottom0100y < ymin
Right0010x > xmax
Left0001x < xmin
Top-left corner1001Above the top and left of the window.

For example, a point above and left of the window has both TOP and LEFT bits set. Its code is 1001.

Step 2: Remember the three decisions

Decision 1 — Trivial accept: If both endpoint codes are 0000, both endpoints are inside. Accept the complete line.

Decision 2 — Trivial reject: If the bitwise AND of the two endpoint codes is not zero, both endpoints are outside on at least one same side. Reject the line.

Decision 3 — Partial clipping: Otherwise, the line may cross the window. Find an intersection with a boundary, replace the outside endpoint with that intersection, calculate its new outcode and repeat the tests.

Very important memory rule: OR = 0 means accept. AND ≠ 0 means reject. If neither condition is true, calculate an intersection.

Step 3: Boundary intersection formulas

Let the endpoints be P1(x1, y1) and P2(x2, y2).

Topy = ymaxx = x1 + (x2 − x1)(ymax − y1)/(y2 − y1)
Bottomy = yminx = x1 + (x2 − x1)(ymin − y1)/(y2 − y1)
Rightx = xmaxy = y1 + (y2 − y1)(xmax − x1)/(x2 − x1)

Cohen–Sutherland pseudocode

1. Calculate the 4-bit outcode for each endpoint.

2. Repeat:
   a. If (code1 OR code2) == 0000:
         Accept the line. Stop.

   b. Else if (code1 AND code2) != 0000:
         Reject the line. Stop.

   c. Otherwise:
         Select an endpoint outside the window.
         Find its intersection with the relevant boundary.
         Replace that endpoint with the intersection.
         Recalculate its outcode.

3. Repeat until the line is accepted or rejected.

6.6 Liang–Barsky line clipping algorithm

Liang–Barsky also clips a line against a rectangular window, but it uses a different method. Instead of assigning region codes and repeatedly replacing endpoints, it writes the line using a parameter u and finds the part of the parameter range that lies inside the window.

Do not mix the two algorithms: Cohen–Sutherland uses outcodes; Liang–Barsky uses a parametric equation.

Step 1: Write the parametric line equation

x = x1 + u(x2 − x1)
y = y1 + u(y2 − y1)
0 ≤ u ≤ 1

When u = 0, we are at the first endpoint. When u = 1, we are at the second endpoint. Values between 0 and 1 represent points along the line.

Step 2: Calculate p and q values

Δx = x2 − x1    Δy = y2 − y1
p1 = −Δx,   q1 = x1 − xmin
p2 = Δx,   q2 = xmax − x1
p3 = −Δy,   q3 = y1 − ymin
p4 = Δy,   q4 = ymax − y1

Step 3: Understand entering and leaving values

  • Start with uenter = 0 and uleave = 1.
  • For p < 0, the line is potentially entering. Update uenter using the larger value.
  • For p > 0, the line is potentially leaving. Update uleave using the smaller value.
  • If p = 0 and q < 0, the line is parallel to that boundary and outside, so reject it.
  • After all four boundaries, if uenter > uleave, reject. Otherwise, calculate the clipped endpoints.

6.7 Polygon clipping — Sutherland–Hodgman algorithm

A polygon is a closed shape made from connected straight edges. Polygon clipping removes the parts outside the clipping window and keeps the part inside.

Sutherland–Hodgman clips a polygon one window boundary at a time. For a rectangular window, process the left, right, bottom and top boundaries in sequence. The output from one boundary becomes the input for the next boundary.

The four cases — learn this table carefully

For each polygon edge, let S be the starting vertex and P be the ending vertex. Check whether each is inside or outside the current clipping boundary.

CaseStart SEnd PWhat is added to output?
1InsideInsideEnd point P.
2InsideOutsideIntersection point only.
3OutsideInsideIntersection point, then P.
4OutsideOutsideNothing.

Easy memory line:

IN → IN: keep the end.

IN → OUT: keep the crossing point.

OUT → IN: keep the crossing point and the end.

OUT → OUT: keep nothing.

Small example against the left boundary

Suppose the left boundary is x = 10. A point is inside this boundary when x ≥ 10.

  • S(15, 20) → P(25, 30): inside to inside, so output P(25, 30).
  • S(15, 20) → P(5, 30): inside to outside, so output the intersection with x = 10.
  • S(5, 20) → P(25, 30): outside to inside, so output the intersection and P(25, 30).
  • S(5, 20) → P(8, 30): outside to outside, so output nothing.

Repeat this for every polygon edge and then for the remaining window boundaries. The final list of output vertices forms the clipped polygon.

6.8 Clipping circles and ellipses

The syllabus also includes circles and ellipses. The same basic rule applies: keep the curve portion inside the clipping window and remove the portion outside. The curve may cross one or more window boundaries, so the intersection points are important.

Circle clipping

A circle with centre (h, k) and radius r has the equation:

(x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2
  • If the circle is fully inside the window, retain the complete circle.
  • If it is fully outside and does not overlap the window, reject it.
  • If it crosses the window, find the intersection points and retain only the arcs inside.

Ellipse clipping

An axis-aligned ellipse with centre (h, k), horizontal radius a and vertical radius b has the equation:

(x − h)2/a2 + (y − k)2/b2 = 1
  • If the ellipse is fully inside, retain it completely.
  • If it does not overlap the window, reject it.
  • If it crosses the window, calculate the boundary intersections and keep the visible arcs.

Do not mix the algorithm names: Cohen–Sutherland and Liang–Barsky are line-clipping algorithms. Sutherland–Hodgman is a polygon-clipping algorithm. Circle and ellipse clipping use their curve equations and boundary intersections.

7.1 What Is Aliasing?

In raster graphics, a continuous geometric shape must be represented using a finite grid of pixels. The computer samples the image at discrete pixel locations. When the sampling process cannot represent the detail or edge accurately, visual errors called aliasing appear.

The most familiar example is a diagonal line. A mathematical diagonal is smooth, but a raster display can only turn whole pixels on or off. The result is a staircase-like edge made from horizontal and vertical pixel steps. These unwanted steps are called jaggies.

Figure 7.1 — A diagonal represented with hard pixel steps appears jagged; partial edge coverage makes the transition smoother.

7.2 What Is Antialiasing?

Antialiasing is a collection of techniques used to reduce the visible artifacts caused by aliasing. It makes the boundaries of lines, circles, curves and filled shapes look smoother by estimating how much of each pixel is covered by the object and assigning an appropriate intensity or colour.

Without antialiasing, an edge pixel is often treated as a simple yes/no decision: fully inside the object or fully outside it. Antialiasing allows an edge pixel to represent partial coverage. A pixel that is 25% covered by a black line should generally look lighter than a pixel that is 100% covered.

Pixel colour = coverage × object colour
       + (1 − coverage) × background colour

Here, coverage is a number between 0 and 1. This is the basic idea for blending an object with its background at an edge. For general colour images, the calculation is applied to colour channels using an appropriate colour-space/blending pipeline.

7.3 Why Does Aliasing Occur?

A raster image is a sampled representation of a scene. The scene may contain edges and details that change more rapidly than the pixel grid can represent. If the sampling rate is too low, high-frequency detail is misrepresented as lower-frequency patterns or jagged boundaries.

In simple words: the image contains more detail than the grid can capture. The grid makes a rough decision about each pixel, and the rough decisions become visible as jagged edges, flicker or patterns.

Sampling and the Nyquist idea

The Nyquist sampling principle says that a band-limited signal must be sampled at more than twice its highest frequency to avoid ambiguity in ideal reconstruction. In image processing, this is a useful principle: detail that changes too rapidly relative to the pixel spacing cannot be represented correctly by that grid.

Sampling frequency > 2 × highest signal frequency

Real geometric edges are abrupt and contain very high-frequency components, so a finite-resolution raster cannot represent an ideal sharp edge perfectly. Antialiasing reduces the unwanted high-frequency content before or during sampling, or estimates pixel coverage using multiple samples.

Figure 7.2 — Sampling a continuous boundary on a coarse grid creates a block-like approximation.

7.4 Basic Principle: Pixel Coverage

A display pixel represents a small square area, not a mathematical point with zero area. If an object covers the entire pixel, coverage is 1. If it covers half the pixel, coverage is 0.5. If it does not touch the pixel, coverage is 0.

Worked numerical example — pixel intensity from coverage

Suppose a black line is drawn on a white background. Represent white intensity as 255 and black as 0. A pixel is covered by the line for 40% of its area. The resulting intensity is:

I = coverage × object intensity
  + (1 − coverage) × background intensity
I = 0.40 × 0 + 0.60 × 255
I = 153

So the edge pixel is grey with intensity 153, rather than fully black (0) or fully white (255). This intermediate value makes the edge look smoother.

7.5 Main Antialiasing Techniques

Several techniques can reduce aliasing. The core ideas taught in introductory computer graphics are increasing the sampling rate, supersampling/postfiltering, area sampling/prefiltering and adjusting pixel intensity near edges. Modern graphics systems also use multisampling and temporal methods.

7.5.1 Increasing the Resolution

The simplest idea is to use a finer pixel grid. When more pixels represent the same physical or geometric edge, each staircase step becomes smaller. The jagged appearance is reduced, although it is not eliminated completely.

Advantage: simple concept and finer detail. Disadvantage: more pixels require more memory, processing and display resources. If the image is later reduced to a smaller resolution without filtering, aliasing can return.

7.5.2 Supersampling (SSAA) / Postfiltering

Supersampling renders or samples the image at a higher resolution than the final display resolution. Several subpixel samples are collected for each final pixel. Those samples are then combined, usually by averaging or by a reconstruction filter, to produce one final pixel value.

For example, instead of taking one sample in a pixel, take four samples arranged in a 2 × 2 grid. If three samples fall inside a black shape and one falls outside on a white background, the estimated coverage is 3/4 = 0.75. The final pixel is therefore mostly black, but not completely black.

Figure 7.4 — Four samples estimate how much of the final pixel is covered by the shape.

Supersampling procedure

  1. Choose a supersampling factor, such as 2× in each dimension.
  2. Create a high-resolution sample grid. A 2× grid in both directions gives 4 subpixel samples per final pixel.
  3. Rasterize the primitive on the higher-resolution grid.
  4. For each final pixel, collect its subpixel sample values.
  5. Average the samples or apply a suitable downsampling filter.
  6. Write the combined value to the final image.
Average intensity = (I₁ + I₂ + ... + Iₙ) / n

7.5.3 Area Sampling (Prefiltering)

Area sampling estimates the fraction of a pixel's area covered by the object. Instead of deciding whether the object passes through one sample point, it considers the overlap between the object and the whole pixel area.

If a pixel is 60% covered by a black object and the remaining 40% is white background, the final intensity is calculated from those area proportions. This is the geometric coverage interpretation of antialiasing.

Coverage = (area of pixel covered by object) / (total pixel area)

For a black object over a white background, if coverage is 0.6:

I = 0.6 × 0 + 0.4 × 255 = 102

Advantage: directly models how much of the pixel is covered. Disadvantage: calculating exact geometric overlap can be complicated for arbitrary shapes, so practical systems often use approximations or sampling.

7.5.4 Weighted Area Sampling / Filtering

Not every sample needs to contribute equally. A weighted filter assigns different weights to nearby samples when combining values. The weights usually depend on distance from the pixel centre and on the chosen filter kernel.

Output pixel = Σ(wᵢ × sampleᵢ) / Σwᵢ

Here \(w_i\) is the weight assigned to sample \(i\). A simple box filter gives equal weight to samples in the box. Other filters give more weight to samples near the centre and less to distant samples.

Filtering can reduce high-frequency image detail before it is represented on the final pixel grid. The filter must be selected carefully: excessive blur removes useful detail, while insufficient filtering leaves aliasing.

7.5.5 Pixel Phasing / Pixel-Position Adjustment

Pixel phasing changes the effective position of a pixel or beam slightly so that the displayed edge can be positioned more accurately relative to the pixel grid. This is a historically important display-oriented technique, especially in descriptions of raster display hardware. It can improve the apparent placement of lines, but it is not as general as modern multisample or coverage-based techniques.

7.5.6 Line Antialiasing

For an antialiased line, pixels near the ideal mathematical line are assigned intensities according to their distance from the line or according to the portion of the pixel covered by the line. Pixels close to the line receive stronger intensity; pixels farther away receive weaker intensity.

One well-known method is Xiaolin Wu's line algorithm. It selects pixels along the major axis and distributes intensity between the two nearest pixels according to the fractional part of the ideal line's minor-axis coordinate. This creates a smoother-looking line than turning on only one pixel per step.

Conceptual example: if the ideal line passes through a column at y = 4.25, it lies 75% of the way from row 4 to row 5. A simplified intensity distribution assigns about 75% contribution to row 4 and 25% to row 5 (depending on the exact distance/coverage convention). The two pixels together represent the line's subpixel position more accurately.

7.5.7 Multisample Antialiasing (MSAA) — Modern Context

MSAA takes multiple coverage samples per pixel, especially to improve polygon edges. In a common implementation, visibility/coverage is tested at multiple sample locations, while shading may be evaluated fewer times than in full supersampling. The sample information is then resolved into a final pixel value.

MSAA can be more efficient than full supersampling when the main problem is geometric edge aliasing. However, it does not automatically remove every kind of aliasing inside textures, shaders or complex shading patterns.

7.5.8 Temporal Antialiasing (TAA) — Modern Context

TAA uses information from multiple frames. A renderer may shift sample positions slightly from frame to frame and combine the current frame with appropriately reprojected information from previous frames. This spreads sampling over time, which can reduce visible jaggies in motion.

TAA can produce ghosting or blur when objects move quickly, when motion vectors are inaccurate, or when previous-frame information no longer matches the current scene. It is a modern rendering technique, not usually a required hand-calculation method in introductory raster graphics.

7.8 Advantages and Disadvantages of Antialiasing

Advantages

  • Reduces staircase-like jagged edges.
  • Improves the appearance of diagonal lines, circles, curves and polygon boundaries.
  • Preserves the apparent smoothness of text and graphical shapes at ordinary viewing sizes.
  • Can reduce flicker and other sampling artifacts when suitable spatial or temporal methods are used.

Disadvantages

  • Requires additional computation, samples, memory or filtering.
  • Some filters can make edges or fine details look blurred.
  • Too few samples may leave visible artifacts; too many samples may be too expensive.
  • Temporal methods can introduce ghosting when motion or history information is incorrect.

For example, with four samples per pixel, if three samples lie inside a black object and one lies outside on a white background, the estimated coverage is 3/4. Supersampling reduces jagged edges but requires extra computation and memory.

5-Mark Answer — Explain area sampling.

Area sampling determines how much of a pixel's area is covered by a graphical object. The pixel intensity is calculated from the covered and uncovered portions. If a black object covers 60% of a pixel over a white background, the final pixel combines 60% black and 40% white. Area sampling represents partial pixel coverage more accurately than a single inside/outside sample, but exact overlap calculations can be complex.

5-Mark Answer — Why is antialiasing required?

Raster displays represent continuous shapes using discrete pixels. When an edge such as a diagonal line does not align with the pixel grid, the selected pixels form visible staircase steps called jaggies. Fine patterns can also produce moiré effects, and moving patterns may flicker. Antialiasing reduces these artifacts by estimating pixel coverage, taking multiple subpixel samples or applying filters. It produces smoother-looking lines, curves and polygon boundaries.

15-Mark Answer — Explain antialiasing and its techniques.

Aliasing occurs when a continuous image is sampled on a finite raster grid at an insufficient or unsuitable rate. It produces jagged edges, moiré patterns, flicker and loss of fine detail. Antialiasing is used to reduce these artifacts by estimating how the image contributes to each pixel.

Pixel coverage: A pixel represents a finite area. If an object covers only part of a pixel, the final intensity is a blend of object and background according to the coverage fraction.

Supersampling: Render at a higher resolution, take multiple subpixel samples for each final pixel and average or filter those samples. It is general and effective but computationally expensive.

Area sampling: Calculate the fraction of each pixel covered by the object and use that fraction to determine pixel intensity. It gives a direct geometric interpretation but exact area calculations can be difficult.

Weighted filtering: Combine samples using weights to reduce high-frequency content before final sampling. The filter must balance smoothness against loss of detail.

Line antialiasing: Assign intensities to neighbouring pixels according to distance or coverage. Xiaolin Wu's method distributes intensity between nearby pixels to represent a line's subpixel position.

Modern methods: MSAA uses multiple coverage samples to smooth geometric edges; TAA combines information across frames. Both have trade-offs and do not remove every possible form of aliasing.

Antialiasing improves visual quality but requires additional processing and may soften fine details if applied too strongly.

7.10 Quick Revision

  • Aliasing: sampling artifacts caused by insufficient or unsuitable sampling.
  • Jaggies: staircase appearance on diagonal or curved edges.
  • Antialiasing: reduces visible aliasing artifacts.
  • Pixel coverage: fraction of a pixel area covered by the object.
  • Supersampling: multiple subpixel samples followed by averaging/filtering.
  • Area sampling: calculate the object-covered fraction of each pixel.
  • Weighted filtering: combine samples with weights.
  • Pixel phasing: adjust effective pixel position.
  • Wu's line algorithm: distributes intensity between neighbouring pixels.
  • MSAA: multiple coverage samples, often focused on geometric edges.
  • TAA: uses information from multiple frames.
  • Trade-off: smoother image quality generally costs more processing or may soften details.

7.11 Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Do not confuse aliasing with antialiasing. Aliasing is the problem; antialiasing is the reduction technique.
  • Do not define antialiasing only as increasing resolution. Supersampling, area sampling and filtering are also important approaches.
  • Do not say an edge pixel must be fully on or fully off. Antialiasing allows intermediate intensities.
  • Do not confuse supersampling with area sampling. Supersampling estimates coverage using multiple samples; area sampling calculates or estimates geometric overlap.
  • Do not claim that more samples completely eliminate aliasing. They reduce it, but the result still depends on sampling and filtering.
  • Do not confuse MSAA and SSAA. MSAA commonly increases coverage sampling, while SSAA can also increase shading/sample evaluation.
  • Do not describe TAA as purely spatial. It combines information across frames.
  • Do not forget the quality-versus-cost trade-off.
