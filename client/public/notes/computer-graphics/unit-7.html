1.1 Meaning of Achromatic Light

Achromatic light is light that has no hue (no colour such as red, green or blue). Its appearance is described by its intensity, producing shades from black through grey to white. In computer graphics, an image represented only by these shades is called a grayscale image.

1.2 Intensity of Achromatic Light

Intensity describes the amount or strength of light. In a grayscale image, the intensity value of each pixel determines how dark or light that pixel appears. A lower value appears darker, while a higher value appears brighter.

8-bit grayscale intensity range

0 = Black

1–254 = Shades of Grey

255 = White

Since 2⁸ = 256, an 8-bit grayscale pixel can represent 256 different intensity levels.

1.3 Characteristics of Achromatic Light

  • No hue: It does not represent a distinct colour.
  • Intensity-based: Its appearance depends on the amount of light.
  • Grayscale shades: It ranges from black to white through intermediate grey levels.
  • Single intensity channel: A grayscale pixel normally stores one intensity value rather than separate red, green and blue values.

1.4 Intensity, Luminance and Brightness

  • Intensity: The amount or strength of light represented at a point.
  • Luminance: A measurable quantity describing light emitted or reflected in a particular direction, weighted according to human visual sensitivity.
  • Brightness: The subjective sensation of how light or dark something appears to a viewer.

These terms are related, but they are not identical. Intensity and luminance are measurable quantities, whereas brightness describes human perception.

1.5 Applications

  • Black-and-white photographs and images.
  • Scanned documents and printed text images.
  • Medical images such as X-rays.
  • Image-processing operations where intensity differences are important.

Exam Answer

Achromatic light is light that has no hue or colour information. It is represented by different intensity levels, ranging from black through shades of grey to white. In computer graphics, an image containing only these shades is called a grayscale image. Each pixel stores an intensity value that determines its appearance. In an 8-bit grayscale image, the values range from 0 to 255: 0 represents black, 255 represents white and the intermediate values represent different shades of grey. Achromatic representation is used in black-and-white photography, document scanning, medical imaging and image processing.

2.1 Meaning of Chromatic Colour

Chromatic colour is colour that has a distinct hue, such as red, green, blue or yellow. It is associated with coloured light and is described using properties such as hue, saturation and intensity or brightness.

Unlike achromatic light, which contains only shades of black, grey and white, chromatic colour carries colour information.

2.2 Main Characteristics of Chromatic Colour

  • Hue: The attribute that identifies the basic colour, such as red, green, blue or yellow. It is commonly associated with the dominant wavelength of the light.
  • Saturation: The purity or vividness of a colour. A highly saturated colour looks strong and vivid, while a less saturated colour looks dull or washed out because it contains a greater neutral component.
  • Intensity / Brightness: Describes the amount or perceived strength of light. Increasing light intensity generally makes the colour appear brighter.

2.3 Hue

Hue tells us which colour we are seeing. For example, red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet are different hues. In many digital colour interfaces, hue is represented as an angle around a colour wheel, usually from 0° to 360°.

Illustrative hue positions in a common colour wheel

0° — Red

60° — Yellow

120° — Green

180° — Cyan

240° — Blue

300° — Magenta

360° — Red again

These are conventional positions used in a hue-wheel representation; they are not a direct measurement of wavelength.

2.4 Saturation

Saturation describes how pure or vivid a colour appears.

  • High saturation: The colour appears strong, vivid and pure.
  • Low saturation: The colour appears dull, pale or washed out.
  • Zero saturation: The colour has no chromatic component and appears as a shade of grey.

For example, bright red is more saturated than a pale, pinkish red.

2.5 Intensity and Brightness

Intensity is related to the amount of light. Brightness is the human perception of how light or dark the colour appears. A colour can keep the same hue while its brightness changes.

For example, dark blue and light blue have a similar hue, but differ in their perceived lightness or brightness.

2.6 Chromaticity

Chromaticity describes the colour quality independently of its overall brightness. In a common description, hue and saturation together specify the chromatic character of a colour, while intensity or luminance describes its light level. The CIE chromaticity diagram is one method used to represent colour qualities and compare colours.

2.7 Difference Between Achromatic and Chromatic Colour

Black, grey and white shadesRed, green, blue, yellow, etc.

Exam Answer

Chromatic colour refers to coloured light that has a distinct hue, such as red, green or blue. It is commonly described by three characteristics: hue, saturation and intensity or brightness. Hue identifies the basic colour and is associated with its dominant wavelength. Saturation indicates the purity or vividness of the colour; a highly saturated colour appears vivid, while a low-saturation colour appears dull or washed out. Intensity refers to the amount of light, while brightness is the perceived lightness or darkness. Hue and saturation together describe the chromatic quality of a colour. Chromatic colour is used in computer graphics to represent coloured images and visual information.

3.1 Meaning of a Colour Model

A colour model is a method of representing colours using numerical components. In raster graphics, each pixel is assigned a colour value, and a colour model defines how that value is described. Different models serve different purposes, such as displaying images, printing and selecting colours.

3.2 RGB Colour Model

RGB stands for Red, Green and Blue. It is an additive colour model mainly used by monitors, televisions and other devices that produce light. A colour is created by combining different intensities of red, green and blue light.

  • Red + Green = Yellow
  • Green + Blue = Cyan
  • Blue + Red = Magenta
  • Full red + green + blue = White
  • No light from any channel = Black

RGB pixel representation

Colour = (R, G, B)

8-bit channel: 0 to 255

Example: (255, 0, 0) is red; (0, 0, 0) is black; (255, 255, 255) is white.

With 8 bits for each channel, an RGB pixel uses 24 bits and can represent 256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 possible channel combinations.

3.3 CMY Colour Model

CMY stands for Cyan, Magenta and Yellow. It is a subtractive colour model, mainly associated with colour printing. It starts with white paper. The inks absorb (subtract) parts of the incident white light, and the remaining reflected light is seen as colour.

  • Cyan absorbs red light.
  • Magenta absorbs green light.
  • Yellow absorbs blue light.

Ideal conversion from normalized RGB to CMY

C = 1 − R

M = 1 − G

Y = 1 − B

R, G and B are normalized to the range 0 to 1.

In theory, combining cyan, magenta and yellow at maximum amounts produces black. In real printing, the result is often not a deep, neutral black.

3.4 CMYK Colour Model

CMYK stands for Cyan, Magenta, Yellow and Key (Black). It extends CMY by adding a separate black channel. It is widely used in printing because dedicated black ink produces clearer text and deeper black areas, while reducing the need to combine large amounts of the other inks.

3.5 HSV Colour Model

HSV stands for Hue, Saturation and Value. It represents colour in terms that are easier for people to understand and select than raw RGB channel values. It is commonly illustrated using a hexagonal cone or cylinder-like colour solid.

  • Hue (H): The basic colour, represented as an angle from 0° to 360°.
  • Saturation (S): The purity or vividness. Zero represents a neutral grey; higher values are more vivid.
  • Value (V): The brightness level in this model. Zero represents black; 1 (or 100%) represents the maximum value.

HSV is useful in colour pickers and image-editing applications where users choose a colour by its appearance.

3.6 HLS / HSL Colour Model

HLS (also commonly written HSL) stands for Hue, Lightness and Saturation. It describes colour using hue, saturation and lightness rather than RGB channel amounts.

  • Hue: Identifies the colour and is represented by an angle.
  • Lightness: Describes how light or dark the colour is. Zero represents black, 1 represents white, and 0.5 is the middle level.
  • Saturation: Describes colour purity. At zero saturation, the colour is a shade of grey.

HLS is often represented by a double cone and is useful for colour selection and adjustment.

3.7 YIQ / YUV Colour Models

YIQ and YUV are colour representations that separate a brightness-related component from colour information. They are associated with television and video systems.

  • Y: Represents luminance or a brightness-related signal.
  • I and Q: In YIQ, represent two colour-difference components.
  • U and V: In YUV, represent colour-difference components.

This separation allows systems to handle brightness and colour information separately. Related representations such as YCbCr are also widely used in digital video and image compression.

Exam Answer

A colour model is a method of representing colours using numerical components. In raster graphics, it specifies how the colour of each pixel is stored or described. Important colour models include RGB, CMY, CMYK, HSV, HLS and YIQ/YUV. RGB is an additive model that combines red, green and blue light and is used in display devices. CMY is a subtractive model used in printing, while CMYK adds a separate black channel. HSV represents colour through hue, saturation and value, and HLS uses hue, lightness and saturation. YIQ and YUV separate brightness-related information from colour information and are associated with television and video systems. Each model is selected according to the application for which colour is being represented or reproduced.

4.1 Meaning of Colour Reproduction

Colour reproduction is the process of producing or displaying colours that resemble the colours of an original object, image or scene. In computer graphics, colour must be reproduced by output devices such as monitors, televisions and printers.

The goal is to make the reproduced colours look as close as possible to the intended colours. Exact matching can be difficult because different devices use different methods and have different colour capabilities.

4.2 Additive Colour Reproduction — RGB

Display devices such as monitors and televisions generally use the RGB additive colour model. They produce colour by emitting red, green and blue light in different intensities.

  • Red, green and blue light combine to create other colours.
  • When all three are at maximum intensity, the result is white.
  • When all three are absent, the result is black.

Example: A computer monitor creates a yellow region by combining red and green light.

4.3 Subtractive Colour Reproduction — CMY / CMYK

Printers generally use the CMYK colour model. It is subtractive because coloured inks absorb some parts of the white light falling on the paper and reflect the remaining light.

  • Cyan, magenta and yellow inks absorb different portions of light.
  • Black ink (K) is added to produce deeper blacks and sharper dark details.
  • The final colour depends on the combination and amount of inks applied to the paper.

Example: A colour photograph printed on paper is reproduced by placing different amounts of cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink.

4.4 Difference Between Display and Printing

Display DevicePrinter
Uses emitted lightUses reflected light from paper
Usually uses RGBUsually uses CMYK
Additive colour mixingSubtractive colour mixing
Full RGB light produces whiteCombination of CMY inks ideally absorbs most light; black ink is added for practical printing

4.5 Factors Affecting Colour Reproduction

  • Device characteristics: Different monitors and printers may reproduce the same numerical colour values differently.
  • Colour range (gamut): Each device can reproduce only a limited range of colours. A colour outside that range cannot be reproduced exactly by that device.
  • Lighting conditions: The light under which a printed image is viewed can change how its colours appear.
  • Media and materials: Paper type, ink and display technology affect the final appearance of colour.
  • Calibration: Adjusting a device to a known reference helps make its colour output more consistent.

4.6 Colour Matching and Colour Management

Colour matching is the process of making colours appear consistent across different devices. A colour-management system helps achieve this by describing device colour behaviour and converting colour values when an image moves from one device or colour space to another.

For example, when an image is transferred from a monitor to a printer, colour management helps convert the display colour values into values that the printer can reproduce more accurately.

Exam Answer

Colour reproduction is the process of producing colours that resemble the colours of an original object, image or scene. In computer graphics, colour is reproduced mainly through display devices and printers. Monitors and televisions generally use the additive RGB model, in which red, green and blue light are combined to produce different colours. Printers generally use the subtractive CMYK model, in which cyan, magenta, yellow and black inks absorb parts of the incident light and produce the required colour on paper. Colour reproduction may vary because devices have different colour ranges, technologies, inks, papers and viewing conditions. Calibration and colour-management systems help maintain more consistent colour reproduction across devices.

5.1 Introduction

Colour is an important part of computer graphics. It makes images more realistic, helps users understand information and improves the appearance of graphical applications. Colour can also be used to distinguish objects, highlight important details and communicate meaning.

5.2 Colour for Realism and Appearance

Colour helps represent the natural appearance of objects and scenes. For example, graphics applications use different colours and shades to represent the sky, grass, water, buildings and other objects. Appropriate colour selection makes a scene easier to recognize and visually understand.

5.3 Colour for Identification and Grouping

Different colours can be assigned to different objects or categories so that users can identify and compare them easily.

  • In maps, different colours can represent roads, rivers, forests and boundaries.
  • In charts, different colours can distinguish data series.
  • In diagrams, colours can separate components or stages of a process.

5.4 Colour for Highlighting and Emphasis

Bright or contrasting colours can draw attention to important information. For example, a warning message may be shown in red, while a selected item may be highlighted using a different background colour.

Colour should be used carefully: too many bright colours can make a display confusing and reduce readability.

5.5 Colour in User Interfaces

Graphical user interfaces use colour to communicate the state and function of controls and information.

  • Different colours can distinguish buttons, menus and selected items.
  • Colour can indicate status, such as success, warning or error.
  • Consistent colour schemes help users understand and navigate an application.

5.6 Colour in Data Visualization

Colour is widely used in charts, graphs and maps to make data easier to interpret.

  • Categorical data: Use distinct colours to identify separate groups.
  • Ordered data: Use a gradual sequence of colours to show low-to-high values.
  • Highlighting: Use a contrasting colour to emphasize an important value or region.

5.7 Important Guidelines for Using Colour

  • Maintain contrast: Text and important objects should be clearly visible against their background.
  • Use colours consistently: The same colour should generally represent the same meaning throughout an application.
  • Avoid excessive colours: Too many colours can make a design difficult to understand.
  • Consider accessibility: Do not communicate important information through colour alone; use labels, shapes or patterns as well.
  • Choose suitable combinations: Colour combinations should remain readable and visually comfortable.

Exam Answer

Colour is used in computer graphics to improve the appearance, realism and understanding of graphical information. It helps represent natural objects and scenes, distinguish different objects or categories, and highlight important details. In graphical user interfaces, colour indicates the state of controls and helps users navigate applications. In data visualization, different colours are used to represent categories, show variations in values and emphasize important information. Effective use of colour requires sufficient contrast, consistency, limited colour choices and consideration of accessibility. Thus, colour improves communication and makes computer graphics more meaningful and easier to interpret.
